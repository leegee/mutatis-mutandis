# Marked Whiteness: Hair, Skin and Eyes in English Writing from the Old English Period to c. 1650, with Welsh and Norse Comparison

*Preliminary research notes/outline, fourth draft. Items in \[square brackets\] need your attention. The title is provisional.*

## 1. Summary

This project asks how a recurring cluster of bodily features, namely unusually white hair, very pale skin and unusual, pale or fiery eyes, was described and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to about 1650. It ends where Thomas White's history of albinism as a medico-scientific and racial category begins (1650–1914), and it asks what repertoire of descriptions and interpretations was available in English when that category started to form.

The principal object is the English textual tradition, which here includes Anglo-Latin writing (4.1). Biblical and classical material is included where it reached English writers. The image of the exceptionally pale body is sparsely attested in any single tradition of the region. The project therefore uses two bounded comparative case studies, one Welsh and one Norse, to test whether the sparseness of the English evidence reflects the tradition or only survival, genre and language (4.3). Irish material is excluded for reasons of scope.

The project combines close literary-historical reading with a bounded computational method: diachronic semantic search that works from both ends of the period, with every machine-retrieved passage checked by hand. The computational work serves the humanities question of locating a rare image, and is not an end in itself.

The project does not assume that earlier religious or literary representations lead to a later medical category. It stops before that category is established, and what it says about later developments is limited to what the pre-1650 evidence does and does not make available.

## 2. Central question and hypotheses

**Question.** How was extreme bodily whiteness described and interpreted in English writing between the Old English period and c. 1650, how did those interpretations vary by period and genre, and what repertoire did this leave available to later writers?

**Working hypotheses (to be tested, not assumed).**

1. Extreme bodily whiteness carried several interpretations from early in its textual history, among them the numinous, the wondrous, the ominous, the monstrous and the natural-philosophical. These coexisted and competed. The project asks which were available to a writer in a given period and genre, and how their relative prominence changed.
2. The project's claim about the later category is limited to the boundary: whether the pre-1650 repertoire contains anything that White's later discourses could have drawn on, and what it does not contain. It makes no claim about what later writers did with the material.
3. The meanings of bodily whiteness are historically constructed, and the project does not assume that the cluster of features is a trans-historical symbol or a natural response to the phenotype.
4. The sparseness of the evidence in English may be partly an effect of survival, genre and language, and the comparative case studies are designed to test this, not to assume it.

A finding that the earlier and later material are largely unrelated, or that matter-of-fact treatments are rare or absent, is a valid result.

## 3. Object of study

"Extreme bodily whiteness" is the project's own term and is not a single feature. White hair, pale skin, pale or fiery eyes, luminous bodies and generic whiteness are separate categories, and part of the project's work is to say how they relate. This section sets out provisional definitions, to be refined in the pilot annotation (year 1).

### 3.1 Core object

A described human or human-like body (including angelic, divine, supernatural and monstrous bodies) in which:

- white or very pale hair co-occurs with at least one further bodily feature from the cluster (very pale skin, pale, red or flame-like eyes); **or**
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a peculiarity of that body in its own right.

Animals are outside the core object, though the whiteness of otherworldly animals in some traditions is noted where it bears on the comparative material (4.3).

### 3.2 Associated features, tracked but not defining

Luminosity or radiance of the body; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; ominous or uncanny reactions to the body. These are annotated as separate tags so that their relationship to the core object can be measured, but their presence alone does not make a passage part of the object.

### 3.3 Excluded

Generic whiteness-as-purity or whiteness-as-holiness symbolism not attached to a described body; white-hair-and-snow/wool similes applied to non-bodies; fair complexion within conventional beauty description.

### 3.4 White hair and age

White hair is conventionally a sign of age, and Daniel 7's Ancient of Days is explicitly aged. The project must therefore separate "white because old" from "white because marked". The approach, to be settled before full retrieval begins:

- Every core-object passage is tagged for age cues (explicit age, implicit age, no age cue, explicit youth).
- The object is defined primarily by co-occurrence with the other features and by the explicit or implicit absence of an age reading. Passages with a strong age cue are retained but analysed separately.
- Revelation 1:14 applies Daniel 7's imagery to a figure who is not presented as aged, and exegetical readings of this transfer (eternity, divinity, wisdom) are part of the evidence for how English writers handled the question. \[Check this against specific commentators and English translations.\]
- The Ancient of Days and the Son of Man are treated as anchors and as reception data (section 7.5), and their interpretations are not assumed to be the object itself.

### 3.5 Controls

Results for the core object are compared with controls chosen to show that the pattern is not simply a generic association of whiteness with holiness or of bodily extremes with wonder: other colour terms applied to hair and skin (red, black, yellow), other bodily extremes (giants, dwarfs, the very old), and white-but-ordinary descriptions (age, snow-covered beards). \[Finalise the control list after the pilot.\]

## 4. Scope and corpora

### 4.1 Primary object: the English textual tradition to c. 1650

English literary, religious, natural-historical and ethnographic writing from the Old English period to c. 1650, together with the biblical and classical material that demonstrably reached English writers. The material falls into four tiers, with the computational weight on the fourth. The depth of treatment is stated for each, so that the breadth does not read as four equal components:

**Anglo-Latin** means Latin writing produced in England and read there, with Latin from elsewhere included where it demonstrably reached English readers (the Vulgate, Pliny, Solinus) or is quoted or translated in English texts. Candidates to examine include Bede and the twelfth-century historians, with Geoffrey of Monmouth and Gerald of Wales as especially relevant to the Welsh case study (4.3) \[check which authors actually contain material\].

Relevant early modern genres include English Bible translations, Holland's 1601 Pliny, voyage and travel collections, natural philosophy and prodigy literature \[check which contain material\].

For the Old English and Anglo-Latin material, searches may be run through translations or other finding aids, which serve only to locate candidate passages. Every passage is checked against the original text, and a translation is never used as evidence (7.4).

### 4.2 Biblical and apocryphal sources

Revelation 1 and Daniel 7 supply the inherited vocabulary, and the English tradition meets them through the Vulgate, the Old English and Middle English biblical material, and the Reformation Bibles. 1 Enoch 106, which presents Noah's luminous, white-haired birth as a cause of paternal fear, offers an earlier and more ambivalent treatment, but was not available in English before Richard Laurence's 1821 translation of the Ethiopic text, and the Greek fragments in Syncellus probably reached English readers only marginally if at all within the period \[verify\]. It is therefore outside the English record. It is used, if at all, as comparative material, to show what an ambivalent early reading looks like and what English writers did not have. Transmission facts of this kind constrain what can be claimed about what English writers could have known at a given date, and the project treats them as constraints.

Classical ethnography and paradoxography (for example Pliny's and Solinus's accounts of the white-haired, pale-eyed people of Caucasian Albania) are included where they reached English readers and are otherwise treated as comparative.

### 4.3 Comparative case studies: Welsh and Norse

**Rationale.** The image of the exceptionally pale body is sparsely attested in any single tradition of the region, so English evidence alone is thin, particularly in the early period. Comparative material is used to test whether this sparseness reflects the tradition or only survival, genre and language. The comparison does not assume a shared culture. Whether the traditions share anything is a question for the case studies, and the best candidate mechanism is Latin and biblical writing known in all of them. Irish material is excluded for reasons of scope, and Hiberno-Latin is used only where it reaches the English tradition through texts that were copied and read in England.

**Number and status.** Two case studies are committed, one Welsh and one Norse, each justified by a specific question the English evidence raises. A third may be added after the year 1 pilot if the evidence calls for it. No more than three will be pursued, and each will claim only a contrast or a structural parallel, never a descent or an equivalence.

Each case study is classified under one of two explicitly separate relations to the English corpus:

1. **Reception evidence.** Material with a documented channel into English writing before c. 1650 is primary evidence for the English tradition.
2. **Comparative material.** Material without a demonstrable channel is used only to sharpen, by contrast or structural parallel, what is specific to the English case.

#### 4.3.1 Welsh case study

The Welsh material is rich in Arthurian and Otherworld narrative, and the Welsh *gwyn* covers whiteness, fairness, blessedness and holiness, so its semantic range is broader than "whiteness" alone. The case study separates two lines of evidence:

- **The Latin and Brut line (reception).** Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae* (c. 1136, in Latin) and its vernacular descendants reached English readers through a documented chain (for example Layamon's *Brut* and later English chronicles and romances) \[confirm the chain and add Malory\]. If whiteness-descriptions occur in this line, they are evidence for the English tradition.
- **The Middle Welsh vernacular line (comparative).** The Mabinogion tales and the other Middle Welsh Arthurian prose are comparative unless a pre-1650 channel into English can be shown. Whether the Welsh romances relate to continental ones, and by what route, is debated, and the project does not take a position on it. \[Check the current scholarship.\]

Cautions specific to the Welsh material:

- **Noise from names and idiom.** *Gwyn* is both a common adjective and a name element (for example in the names of figures such as Gwyn ap Nudd and Gwenhwyfar), and white is a conventional epithet in many contexts. Retrieval will therefore produce many hits that are not the object as defined in section 3. The controls (3.5) and the exclusion of generic whiteness (3.3) are essential here. \[Check specific passages. I am recalling these names from memory.\]
- **Animals and bodies.** Welsh Otherworld narrative includes white animals (for instance the white, red-eared hounds associated with Annwfn), which the object excludes (3.1). The relation between the two is noted but not conflated.
- **Dating.** The Middle Welsh texts survive in manuscripts of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries onwards, and their composition dates are often uncertain and debated \[confirm\]. The project makes no chronological claims about the Welsh material beyond what the evidence supports.
- **Translation.** The prototype's retrieval of Welsh material from the query "hair as white as wool" probably comes from a nineteenth-century or modern translation, in which case it lies outside the period for the English tradition and is evidence for the translator's vocabulary, not for the Welsh text. \[Say which translations the hits come from.\] It is useful as a pointer to passages, which are then read in the Middle Welsh.
- **Language competence.** The researcher does not read Middle Welsh. The case study depends on dictionaries (*Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru*), on the available Middle Welsh prose corpus \[confirm which corpus, e.g. Rhyddiaith Gymraeg 1300–1425\], on translations and scholarship as finding aids, and on a Welsh-reading advisor or collaborator for the passages that matter. \[Identify an advisor, and decide how much of this is realistic.\]

#### 4.3.2 Norse case study

The Norse case study centres on Baldr. Snorri's pale Baldr is often argued to owe something to Christian imagery, and the earlier poetic Edda presents a less strongly pale figure under the same name. Establishing independence from Christian sources is outside the project's scope. The English reception of the image is largely after c. 1650, so the case study is comparative. It is justified only if the English evidence raises a question that the Norse material can clarify, and Baldr and the Ancient of Days are not presented as two independent witnesses to one complex. ### 4.4 Twentieth-century appropriation

Twentieth-century reception lies well outside the period but bears on how the project is framed. Norse whiteness-symbolism was appropriated by racial-nationalist and occult movements (see Goodrick-Clarke on the occult roots of Nazism and Van Deusen on Old Norse reception history) \[confirm the specific works\]. This is one reason the project treats the meanings of bodily whiteness as historically constructed and not inherent. Its aim is to historicise such symbolism, and it does not endorse any use of it.

### 4.5 The interface with White's thesis

Thomas White's 2012 Manchester thesis, *"Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours": A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650–1914*, covers the period after the cut-off, and the project defers to it for everything after 1650. The project takes from White the history of the medico-scientific category, including the early uses of the term "albino" and its precursors, and does not repeat that history. In return it offers an account of the repertoire of descriptions and interpretations available in English by c. 1650, set out so that White's readers can see what was already in circulation and what was not.

Two practical measures manage the boundary:

- **An interface window (c. 1650–1700).** Retrieval is extended to c. 1700 (within EEBO's range) only to find the earliest English uses of "albino" and its precursors, which anchor the backward search chain (7.2). Passages from the window are not analysed as part of the corpus. The term is thought to enter English around 1700, via Portuguese uses in the 1650s or thereabouts \[confirm the first English attestation in the OED and White\]. If the first English uses fall after EEBO's range, a small targeted lookup in ECCO supplies the anchor, and the corpus is unchanged.
- **Reading White for the pre-1650 background.** His thesis probably discusses earlier material in its introduction, and the project should check what it already says before duplicating it. \[Read the thesis's opening chapters and note overlaps.\]

## 5. Position within existing scholarship

White's thesis establishes how albinism emerged as a medico-scientific category through travel writing, natural history, medical classification and racial discourse (4.5). The present project asks a different, earlier question: what is the longer history of the cultural semantics surrounding extreme bodily whiteness in English up to that point? It does not assume that White's category inherits earlier material.

Charlotte Baker's work on the representation of albinism in Francophone and African contexts informs the project in two ways. It shows that the meaning of extreme bodily whiteness is produced locally within specific discursive and social conditions, which disciplines the project's own historical claims. And her grounding in critical disability studies offers a model for keeping embodiment, stigma and the lived consequences of visible difference in view even where the evidence is textual.

For the early material, the project draws on Alaric Hall's work on Old English supernatural beings and their radiance and whiteness (his PhD thesis and, I believe, its published form, *Elves in Anglo-Saxon England*, 2007) \[confirm the relevant chapters and that the book is the same work\] and on Jackson Crawford's work on Old Norse colour terminology \[confirm the specific work\]. \[Identify the main Welsh and Arthurian scholarship for 4.3.1.\]

## 6. Research questions

1. **Construction and change.** How is extreme bodily whiteness described and interpreted in English writing (including Anglo-Latin) from the Old English period to c. 1650, and how do the interpretations available to writers, including their valence (blessed or holy versus cursed or unholy), vary across period and genre?
2. **Discourses.** How do religious, literary, natural-philosophical and ethnographic discourses treat visibly marked bodies in this period, and where, if anywhere, are symbolic descriptions applied to actual bodily conditions?
3. **Selection, salience and the mundane phenotype.** Textual survival favours encounters that provoked reaction. Does the corpus preserve evidence of matter-of-fact encounters with the phenotype, and how do such depictions compare with symbolic ones? (See 7.8.)
4. **The repertoire at the boundary.** What repertoire did English writing offer by c. 1650, which channels (quotation, allusion, shared authorities, genre conventions) link earlier representations to later ones, and what does the earlier evidence not contain that White's discourses later required?
5. **Comparison.** Do the Welsh and Norse case studies suggest that the sparseness of the English evidence reflects the tradition, or survival, genre and language, and what do they clarify about what is specific to the English case?
6. **Method.** Can a two-ended diachronic semantic search, anchored on physical description at one end and on the earliest terms for such bodies at the other, retrieve passages that describe similar bodies without presupposing that they are equivalent or connected?

## 7. Methodology

### 7.1 Overall design

Computational retrieval identifies candidate passages across the corpus at scale. Close reading then interprets them. The aim is to find large-scale patterns in how the object is described and interpreted that conventional reading cannot easily survey, and to subject those patterns to detailed literary and historical analysis.

### 7.2 Lexicon-chain search from both ends, and the convergence principle

A seed lexicon is built from two directions:

- **Forward from the earliest material.** Descriptive anchors drawn from Revelation 1, Daniel 7 and comparable early passages, namely white hair ("like wool", "like snow"), pale skin and pale, flame-like or red eyes. The lexicon is expanded slice by slice through collocates and nearest neighbours.
- **Backward from the early modern end.** The earliest English terms for such bodies, including "albino" and its precursors if they occur in the interface window, and otherwise the descriptive and ethnographic vocabulary found there (to be compiled from White's thesis and from corpus evidence), expanded backward in the same way.

**Convergence is not connection.** That the two chains overlap, and where, shows only that their vocabularies overlap in the corpus. It is a prompt for close reading and is not evidence of historical connection. Shared stock similes, translation artefacts, generic whiteness vocabulary and the drift of embedding neighbourhoods can all produce overlap without any historical relationship. The project treats the following as evidence for a connection: a documented channel (citation, quotation, allusion, a shared authority), shared genre conventions, or demonstrable reception. Overlap without any of these is reported as coincidence or left undetermined. This principle applies to every stage of the analysis, and it governs the comparative case studies in particular, where similar images in different traditions are not evidence of a common origin. Because both ends of the chain now lie inside the early modern period, the convergence test is weaker than in a design with a modern end, and the project does not claim more for it than that.

Anchoring on description reduces anachronism, but the category of "albino-like" is the project's own construction and is treated as such.

### 7.3 Passage-level retrieval

Because the features are usually spread across clauses, retrieval works at sentence or short-passage level, with each feature (hair, skin, eyes) annotated separately.

### 7.4 Models, translations and the evidence rule

**The rule.** Models and translations are finding aids. The evidence is always the passage in the original language. Every passage retrieved by a model or through a translation is mapped back to the original and read there before it is annotated or interpreted.

- **Early modern English.** MacBERTh, a historical language model whose coverage is roughly 1450 to 1950 \[confirm\], is used for the whole of the c. 1450–1700 corpus. No modern model is needed. Time slices are sized by corpus volume, with overlapping windows and genre balancing, since the phenomenon is rare and religious texts would otherwise dominate the early slices.
- **Anglo-Latin.** Latin is the best-resourced of the pre-1450 languages. A Latin or multilingual model will be tested on medieval material in year 1, since such models may be weighted towards classical Latin \[confirm availability and licensing of corpora\]. Where a model proves usable, retrieval can be systematic. Where it does not, lexical search with the *Dictionary of Medieval Latin from British Sources* is the fallback.
- **Old English, Middle English, Middle Welsh and Old Norse.** Comparable models are weak or absent, so retrieval is lexical and collocational on annotated editions and dictionaries, followed by close reading. No claim of systematic coverage is made for the case-study languages.
- **Translation bias.** Searching through translations has a built-in bias. A query such as "hair as white as wool" will preferentially retrieve translators who rendered the original in Bible-flavoured English, because they were echoing Daniel and Revelation, and the search would then find its own anchor in the translation and look like evidence of a shared image. The project therefore uses an independent route that does not go through English, namely lexical search in the original language, so that the recall of the translation-based route can be estimated.

### 7.5 Quotation and reception

Biblical quotation is not filtered out. Each occurrence is tagged as quotation, allusion or independent use, and quotation is treated as reception data in its own right. Statistical summaries report results with and without quotations.

### 7.6 Controls

See 3.5. Results for each control are reported alongside the core object, and differences are interpreted case by case. The controls are especially important for the Welsh material, where *gwyn* is frequent and often not the object (4.3.1).

### 7.7 Validation

A hand-annotated sample of retrieved passages, double-annotated where possible, checks the retrieval against close reading, and gives precision estimates for each period. Disagreements are reported. The annotated sample is a research output in its own right.

### 7.8 Methodology note: the recording of reaction

The surviving record may preserve reactions to the phenotype rather than ordinary encounters with it. Each occurrence is therefore annotated on two dimensions: whether the text records an explicit reaction at all, and, where it does, its character and intensity (astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust, theological or natural-philosophical interpretation). Special attention goes to matter-of-fact descriptions, which are the best test of the selection effect. The frequency of reaction in the surviving record can be measured directly. The probability that an unremarkable encounter was recorded at all can only be approached indirectly, and the project keeps these two questions apart when interpreting counts. The same caution applies to the interpretation of absences in the comparative material, where an absence may reflect survival, genre or language and not the tradition.

## 8. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who himself has albinism. This informs the choice of questions and brings an awareness of the consequences of historical representation. It does not determine the meanings the research identifies. The project does not assume that albinism or extreme bodily whiteness has inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that particular writers, communities and traditions have attached to visibly marked bodies. The methodology is explicitly reflexive, with interpretative claims grounded in textual, linguistic and historical evidence, and with evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own judgement kept distinct.

### 9.1 Beneficiaries and impact

This project examines how extreme bodily whiteness, including the features now associated with albinism, was represented in English writing before the category of albinism took shape. Existing scholarship points mainly to representations of monstrosity, wonder and medical curiosity. The project asks a different question: what range of representations survives in the record, including veneration and matter-of-fact treatment, and what can the record tell us about ordinary lives?

The main beneficiaries beyond academia are people with albinism and their families. Albinism affects roughly 1 in 17,000 people in Europe and North America \[confirm and cite\], yet the cultural history available to them is largely one of stigma. A fuller historical picture, including any positive images the record preserves, could support education and public understanding.

The project will release an openly accessible, annotated collection of the passages it identifies, together with a plain-language summary of its findings. It will seek to consult albinism organisations, such as Standing Voice and national support groups, on how results are presented, and it will share findings with them.

## 10. Risks

- **Sparse data.** The phenomenon is rare. Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, seed-lexicon expansion, and hand annotation in place of statistical claims where counts are low.
- **Model coverage and noise.** OCR errors and spelling variation affect early printed text. Mitigation: use of an EEBO-trained model, MacBERTh, and minimal normalisation.
- **Scope.** Four English tiers and two comparative case studies is a lot, and breadth is the main criticism this proposal has already received. Mitigation: the depth of treatment is fixed for each tier (4.1), the computational weight is on c. 1450–1650, the case studies are capped at two committed and one optional (4.3), and Irish material is excluded. \[Review after year 1, and be prepared to cut Middle English, narrow the Anglo-Latin selection or drop the second case study.\]
- **Language competence.** The researcher reads Old English and Scandinavian languages but not Middle Welsh or, perhaps, medieval Latin at research level \[confirm\]. Mitigation: a Welsh-reading advisor, and a Latin model and dictionaries with help from a medievalist.
- **Dating of the Welsh material.** The composition dates of the Middle Welsh texts are uncertain. Mitigation: no chronological claims beyond the evidence, and comparison made by contrast and structural parallel.
- **Boundary effects.** Ending at c. 1650 means the project cannot say what became of its material. Mitigation: the interface window and chapter (4.5), and deference to White for later developments.
- **Dependence on White.** The handover relies on his account being accurate and complete for its period. Mitigation: read it critically, and state in the thesis what is taken from it.
- **Defining the object.** The age problem (3.4) and the line between the core object and associated features may not resolve cleanly. Mitigation: settle the working definition in the year 1 pilot, keep borderline cases as tagged categories, and report results with and without them.
- **Over-reading convergence.** Overlap between lexicon chains, or between traditions, may be mistaken for historical connection. Mitigation: the convergence principle (7.2), controls and validation against close reading.
- **Circularity.** Anchors shape results. Mitigation: two-ended search, controls and validation against close reading.
