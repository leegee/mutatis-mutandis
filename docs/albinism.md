# Representing Visible Difference: Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Eyes in English Writing, c. 700-1914

*Preliminary research outline, revised working draft. The title remains provisional. Items in square brackets require verification or a decision.*

## 1. Starting point and contribution

Thomas White's thesis, *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650-1914* (University of Manchester, 2011), is the essential starting point. White traces the changing significance of albinism through travel writing, natural history, medical classification, biological research and the self-representation of people with albinism. He shows that albinism was not a timeless condition awaiting scientific discovery, but a category whose meanings and social consequences developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity.

This project inquires of an earlier and longer period. Its central working hypothesis is that a specific cluster of biblical imagery - principally the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9 and the Son of Man in Revelation 1:13-14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition - supplied English writers with a durable repertoire for describing extraordinarily pale or radiant human or human-like bodies. The research investigates how that repertoire was received, adapted, transformed or displaced in English writing from the Old English period to 1914, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism.

The project does not presume that the biblical imagery constitutes an unbroken tradition, that later descriptions are straightforward descendants of it, or that the material culminates in modern albinism. Whether any such relationships exist, and on what evidence they can be claimed, is part of the research. It draws on literary history attending to stigma, embodiment and the consequences of representation without assuming that the descriptions examined concern disabled or medicalised bodies.

## 2. Summary, central question and hypotheses

**Central question**

*How was a cluster of biblical imagery describing extraordinarily pale or radiant bodies received, adapted and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to 1914, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism?*

The research combines close reading of the earliest texts and their exegetical contexts with a bounded computational method: passage-level semantic retrieval across large historical corpora. Searches proceed in two directions: forwards from the biblical anchors and early vernacular descriptions, and backwards from the modern and early-modern vocabulary of albinism. Retrieved passages are examined in their textual, linguistic and historical contexts. Computational retrieval is used as a means of finding evidence that conventional reading may miss, not as an independent research objective.

Two working hypotheses structure the investigation with the intention of being testable and potentially disprovable.

- **H1: Period-specific meanings and the fortunes of the biblical repertoire.** Prior to about 1550, English descriptions of extraordinarily pale bodies attach mainly to the numinous, otherworldly or wondrous, drawing substantially (though not exclusively) on the biblical and exegetical imagery of white hair, radiant or wool-like appearance, and associated features. Natural-historical and medical explanations emerge chiefly in the seventeenth century. H1 would be challenged by substantially earlier matter-of-fact or naturalistic accounts that show no contact with the biblical repertoire, or by evidence that the numinous/biblical framings persist unchanged into the medical period.

- **H2: Limited reception into later naturalistic accounts.** Later naturalistic and medical accounts of bodies described in terms associated with albinism draw principally on classical, travel and medical authorities rather than on the earlier biblical, religious and literary imagery. H2 would be challenged by demonstrable chains of quotation, allusion, translation or shared interpretive tradition connecting the two bodies of material. Evidence of both inheritance and discontinuity will be considered.

These hypotheses do not presume a continuous tradition. Earlier and later representations may prove largely unrelated; matter-of-fact descriptions may be scarce; and apparent patterns may result from conventional imagery, textual transmission, selection bias, or the accidents of survival. The biblical material is treated as a historically available repertoire whose actual influence must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

## 3. Research questions

1. **Representation and historical change.** How is extraordinary paleness described in English writing, and what meanings attach to it across periods and genres including religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical and medical writing? How do its moral and emotional associations vary?

2. **The body and its interpretation.** How do writers distinguish or conflate unusual hair, skin, eyes, bodily radiance as well as any functions attributed to the eyes, such as better sight at night? When do these features signify holiness, wonder, danger, monstrosity, physical difference or a condition requiring explanation? What can be learned from descriptions that provoke no explicit reaction?

3. **Historical relationship and discontinuity.** Where can specific connections be demonstrated between earlier representations and later accounts of albinism? What roles do quotation, allusion, translation, shared authorities and genre conventions play? Where does the evidence indicate discontinuity or leave the relationship unresolved?

4. **The English tradition in context.** What do biblical, classical and other sources contribute where a demonstrable channel of reception exists? What can bounded comparison clarify about the specificity (or otherwise) of the English reception of the biblical repertoire?

## 4. Object of study and scope

### 4.1 Defining extraordinary paleness and a note on terminology

"Extraordinary paleness" is an analytical term developed for this project, not a historical category assumed to have been recognised consistently. It is used in preference to "whiteness" to avoid confusion with any racial sense of that word in modern scholarship, and in preference to "albinism" to avoid presupposing a medical category. Historical sources' own terms, including "white", "hoary" and "wan", will be quoted and analysed in context. Outside quotation, the project uses "person with albinism" and avoids "albino" as a noun. Historical terms in the later corpus, such as "white negro" and "kakerlak", are racialised or offensive; they will be analysed as evidence of their historical contexts and flagged as such in any output.

The core object comprises descriptions of human or human-like bodies, including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures, in which:

- white or very pale hair occurs alongside at least one further marked bodily feature, such as unusually pale skin or distinctive pale, red or flame-like eyes; or
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a bodily peculiarity in its own right.

Associated features will be recorded separately: bodily luminosity; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor, as in descriptions of Grendel's eyes; functions ascribed to the eyes, such as better sight at night; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; and observers' reactions. These may illuminate a description without establishing that it belongs to the core object.

Excluded are generic symbolism of white, such as purity or holiness, when not attached to a described body; similes of snow or wool applied to non-bodies; and descriptions of conventional fair complexion. Borderline cases will be retained in separate categories where excluding them would obscure a potentially significant relationship.

**Known confounds.** "White-headed", "towhead", "flaxen," "hoary" and similar terms often describe the grey- and fair-haired and may pollute retrieval. Lexical work will draw on Biggam's studies of Old English colour terms, the *Thesaurus of Old English* and the *Historical Thesaurus of English*, alongside contextual and collocational analysis.

### 4.2 White hair, age and markedness

Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13–14, together with their Latin exegetical tradition, form the foundational repertoire whose reception in English writing the project investigates.

White hair may be associated with age, wisdom, purity, radiance and divine authority, but these associations are not interchangeable or self-evident. Patristic interpretations of Daniel 7:9 illustrate their plurality. Jerome distinguishes the symbolism of the Ancient of Days' apparent age from that of his wool-like hair: he interprets the figure's age as signifying the maturity of divine judgement, and the comparison of the hair to pure wool as signifying the purity and impartiality of that judgement (*Commentarii in Danielem*, on Daniel 7:9; translated by Gleason L. Archer, 2024).

Origen, in *Homilies on Numbers* 27.12.5, discusses Libnah, whose name he interprets as "whitening", and connects the Ancient of Days' wool-like hair with the splendour of true light and the brightness of heavenly visions (translated by Thomas P. Scheck, 2009). Cyril of Jerusalem, in *Catechetical Lecture* 15.21, explains that the Father's white clothing and wool-like hair are described "after the manner of men". He connects the imagery with Isaiah 1:18, where sins are made "white as snow and wool," interpreting it in terms of forgiveness and sinlessness. Bede's *Expositio Apocalypseos* (Book I, chapter 3), commenting on Revelation 1:14, interprets the whiteness of the head as signifying "the antiquity and immortality of majesty" (*antiquitas et immortalitas maiestatis*). He also interprets the wool and snow imagery in relation to the righteous and the innumerable multitude of the elect.

These readings show that Christian interpreters assigned different, sometimes overlapping theological meanings to the imagery. Their significance for English writing must be established through evidence of transmission and reception, rather than presumed from the existence of the commentaries.

The analysis of Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13–14, and of their Latin exegetical tradition, therefore constitutes the necessary starting point for tracing reception. The analysis will begin with the Aramaic wording of Daniel 7:9, examining the description of the Ancient of Days, the imagery of white clothing and wool-like hair, and the relationship between these features and the surrounding vision of divine judgement and sovereignty. Relevant parallels elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible and ancient Near Eastern traditions will be considered where they clarify the wording or imagery, without assuming that they establish a single inherited convention.

The project will then examine the Greek versions of Daniel and the reuse of the imagery in Revelation 1:13-14, asking what was preserved, reinterpreted or potentially altered in transmission. The subsequent Latin exegetical tradition will be explored through selected commentaries, beginning with Bede and considering relevant interpretations of Daniel and Revelation. The analysis will trace demonstrable transmission into English biblical and literary writing, distinguishing the availability of a text or interpretation from evidence of its reception.

Each relevant passage will be annotated for explicit or implicit age cues, explicit youth, and the absence of a discernible age cue.

### 4.3 Controls

Controls will test whether apparent patterns are specific to extraordinary paleness or reflect general literary convention. They will include other hair and skin colours (red, black, yellow and green); other bodily extremes (giants, dwarves and the very elderly); and ordinary descriptions of white, hoary and grey hair, including snow-covered beards. The final set will be determined during the pilot. The purpose is interpretive as well as quantitative.

### 4.4 Corpus and periods

The primary corpus is English writing from the Old English period to 1914, organised into tiers as below. These are presented in addition to the foundational examination of the biblical material.

1. **Old English, c. 700-1150.** Lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading of the original texts. Starting points include *Wonders of the East* and the wider marvellous-peoples tradition, Grendel's eyes, and *ælfscȳne* and related vocabulary. Readings will use existing scholarship where appropriate and detailed analysis of the Old English where necessary, following the philological approach exemplified by Alaric Hall.

2. **Middle English, c. 1150-1450:** Targeted lexical searches and close reading, rather than systematic computational coverage. Particular attention will be paid to encyclopaedic and historical works that may preserve or transmit earlier accounts of unusual bodily characteristics. A priority is Bartholomaeus Anglicus's *De proprietatibus rerum*, Book XV, chapter 7 ("De Albania"), and John Trevisa's Middle English translation, to investigate the reception of descriptions of the *Albani* associated with Isidore of Seville's *Etymologiae* IX.2.65. The account describes white hair, distinctive eyes and unusually good night vision. Whether the relevant details survive in the English translation, and how they are rendered, remains to be verified. Other possible channels include Middle English biblical translations, Mandeville's *Travels*, and selected historical or encyclopaedic works, including Higden's *Polychronicon* where relevant passages can be established. The scale and availability of these materials will be assessed during the pilot study.

3. **c. 1450-1650.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus will be EEBO-TCP, providing a substantial body of searchable early printed English texts across religious, literary, historical, natural-philosophical and medical writing. This period is central to the investigation of how descriptions of the subject were interpreted across different genres, and whether older religious or literary imagery coexisted with, or was reinterpreted through, emerging natural-historical explanations of bodily variation. Semantic retrieval will identify candidate passages that may not be found through searches for a single historical or modern label; lexical and collocational searches will provide complementary routes into the material. Retrieved passages will be examined in context, with attention to genre, date, source relationships and the distinction between descriptions of bodily features and their symbolic or narrative functions.

4. **c. 1650-1914.** This period will provide a comparative corpus for investigating the emergence and development of natural-historical and medical accounts of people described in terms associated with "albinism", alongside the continued representation of unusual bodily whiteness in literary and other writing. ECCO will provide the initial early-period resource, a suitable nineteenth-century collection will be selected during the pilot, with candidates including HathiTrust, Gale's nineteenth-century collections, texts of the Wellcome Collection and the *Philosophical Transactions*. CLMET may serve as a supplementary test set but is too small to represent the nineteenth century on its own. Thomas White's study will provide a starting point for identifying relevant authors, texts and passages, while the corpus investigation will test and extend that evidence rather than assume that his account exhausts the available material.

The computational method will be evaluated across periods, but the historical analysis will not be evenly distributed. Interpretive effort will be concentrated on Old and Middle English material and the early modern period before 1650. The later corpus will serve both as a historical comparator and as a test of whether passage-level retrieval can recover relevant material identified independently through existing scholarship. The relationship between retrieval performance and the changing language, genres and explanatory frameworks of the periods will be assessed rather than assumed.

### 4.5 What is core, contextual and excluded

The project is deliberately bounded. A three-year doctorate cannot provide complete coverage of all the traditions that touch this topic; breadth is reluctantly seen a risk to be managed rather than a virtue.

**Core (committed).**

* Biblical and early exegetical repertoire (Daniel 7, Revelation 1, selected Latin commentaries with emphasis on Bede) and the investigation of its transmission into English.
* Old English close reading (tier 1), with particular attention to possible contact with or independence from the biblical imagery.
* Middle English channel studies (tier 2), especially encyclopaedic and historical works that may transmit, adapt or ignore the repertoire.
* Early modern retrieval and close reading before 1650.
* Backward searches from the later vocabulary of albinism.
* Assessment of reception, transformation and discontinuity (H1 and H2).

**Contextual (bounded, in service of the core).**

- Biblical and exegetical reception. Daniel 7 and Revelation 1, with one case study of Bede (4.2), forming part of the Old English or Middle English work. Apocryphal comparison is limited to the *Similitudes of Enoch* (1 Enoch 46) and the birth of Noah (1 Enoch 106), where Noah's luminous, unusual body provokes paternal fear. Reception will be traced only through demonstrable channels, including Richard Laurence's English translation of 1821 and later revisions, and R. H. Charles's edition of 1893 and later revisions. The Genesis Apocryphon's version of Noah's birth (Qumran) is comparative only.

- Limited Classical ethnography and paradoxography. Pliny and Solinus on the white-haired, pale-eyed Albani, including the claim of better sight at night, and on the *leucaethiopes*. These will be considered where their reception in English can be shown, chiefly through the Middle English encyclopaedic channels identified above.

- Limited Latin and Anglo-Norman writing from England. A small control set, not a systematic search, will test whether conventions for recording anomalous bodies differ from those of English-language writing. One example is the twelfth-century Latin reports of the Green Children of Woolpit. The exclusion of Latin from the primary corpus is a practical limit and will be acknowledged as such.

- Comparative Welsh, Irish and Norse material (as currently defined in 4.6), treated strictly as reception and contrast rather than common inheritance.

**Comparative (one chapter, reception and translation only).**

Welsh, Irish and Norse material, treated in 4.6. This chapter is limited to a short list of cases and depends on specialist editions, translations and secondary research. The researcher's language competence will be stated plainly: Old Norse is manageable through existing knowledge of modern Scandinavian languages, while Welsh and Irish material will require reliance on existing scholarship and specialist assistance. This will be the first strand reduced if the schedule requires.

**Not undertaken.**

Systematic coverage of non-English originals; computational coverage of Old and Middle English; a second general history of albinism after 1650; and the twentieth century (see 4.7). The annotated dataset is a secondary output and will be released only where rights allow.

**Review point.**

At the end of Year 1, assess the productivity of the biblical hypothesis. The scale of the Middle English and comparative components will be fixed on the basis of pilot results. Anything infeasible will be dropped rather than compressed.

### 4.6 On the Welsh, Irish and Norse traditions: reception and comparison

The comparative cases will be selected for their relevance to the representation and interpretation of extraordinary bodily whiteness. Each will provide evidence of the core phenomenon, offer a useful contrast, or raise a specific interpretive question. Radiance, supernatural beauty, white clothing and symbolic whiteness will not be treated as equivalent to unusually white hair or pale skin, though their relationship to bodily description may clarify how whiteness is represented. Names and epithets will likewise be distinguished from explicit descriptions of appearance. The aim is to investigate meaningful similarities without presuming that superficially comparable figures belong to a single tradition or represent the same phenomenon.

**Welsh.** In *Culhwch ac Olwen*, Olwen is described with hair yellower than broom and skin whiter than the foam of the wave. This may represent conventional fair complexion and will be tested as a control rather than assumed to be a core case. The white hounds with red ears in *Pwyll* are animals and fall outside the definition, though they may illuminate the pairing of white and red as an Otherworld marker. Gwyn ap Nudd, the Very White Witch and other figures are candidates only. The analysis will examine the semantic range of *gwyn/gwen*-whiteness, fairness, brightness and blessedness-and whether its use associates with beauty, heroism, supernatural status or liminality. It will not assume that Christianity created these associations, but will consider inherited vocabulary, Christian symbolism and the transformation of vernacular narrative as interacting possibilities.

**Irish.** Fionn/Find is a potential comparative case, subject to verification of the source and circumstances of the reported whitening of his hair. The analysis will distinguish an explicit description of bodily change from a name or epithet. Macpherson's Ossianic poems of the 1760s provide a route into English-language reception, but will be treated as evidence of eighteenth-century literary reception rather than as transparent evidence of earlier Irish tradition.

**Norse.** Snorri describes Baldr as so fair of face and so bright that light shines from him, and names a very white plant after his brow. This is evidence of radiance and beauty, not necessarily white hair or skin. It will be considered alongside *Vǫlundarkviða* (stanza 2), with "hvítan hals Völundar" (the white neck of Völundr), and *Þrymskviða* (stanza 15), where Heimdallr is "hvítastr ása" (the whitest of the Æsir). Relations between Norse and Christian imagery, the etymology of Baldr's name, differences between the poetic accounts and Snorri's account, and the semantics of the underlying terms are debated and will be addressed through the secondary literature (see Section 5), supplemented by close reading where the scholarship leaves questions open. Reception evidence includes Percy's English translation (1770) of Mallet's history of Denmark, later Eddic translations and the medievalist writing of William Morris.

Reception evidence will comprise texts demonstrably available to English readers. Translated vocabulary will be treated as evidence of translators' choices, not automatically as evidence of the source tradition, and will be examined in context. Comparative material will clarify specific problems raised by the English evidence; claims of common inheritance will require evidence beyond resemblance of vocabulary or description.

### 4.7 Later appropriation

Twentieth-century racial-nationalist and occult appropriations of Norse and related imagery lie outside the period of study. They matter as a reason to historicise meanings of whiteness rather than treat them as inherent. Goodrick-Clarke and Van Deusen provide relevant research. The project does not endorse such appropriations.

## 5. Existing scholarship

White's thesis is the principal point of departure for the history of albinism as a category. This research uses it to identify sources and debates while avoiding a second general history of the same period. The difference is one of emphasis and object: the project begins from a broader and earlier set of bodily descriptions and asks how their meanings developed, whether or not they were ever associated with albinism.

Charlotte Baker's work on representations of albinism in Francophone and African contexts demonstrates the importance of examining representation within particular social and discursive settings and engages critical disability studies. This informs the project's treatment of stigma and embodiment. For Old English, Alaric Hall's *Elves in Anglo-Saxon England* (2007) offers a framework for supernatural beings, radiance and unusual bodies. Jackson Crawford's 2014 thesis, *The Historical Development of Basic Color Terms in Old Norse-Icelandic*, provides a comparative resource where it bears on a defined problem.

The literature review will also establish the relevant scholarship on biblical transmission, reception, medieval and early modern descriptions of the body, historical colour terminology (including Biggam), and the emergence of medical understandings of albinism. These strands will be organised around the central question rather than presented as separate surveys.

## 6. Computational methodology

The technical work builds on my background as a professional software developer, with more than 25 years' experience, and an MSc in Knowledge-based Systems from the Centre for Cognitive Science at the University of Sussex. This experience has enabled me to develop a working prototype for passage-level retrieval across historical texts. The proposed research does not depend on developing a new language model or retrieval architecture from scratch, its methodological contribution lies in evaluating how existing techniques can support the discovery and interpretation of historically significant evidence.

### 6.1 Design

Computational methods will identify candidate passages and patterns that are difficult to survey through reading alone, alongside the study of pre-determined texts. Each passage will then be interpreted in relation to its wording, genre, authorship, context and relationship to other sources. Embedding similarity, keyword frequency and cluster membership guide the investigation; they do not establish historical meaning. Claims will rest on the interpretation of passages in their textual and historical contexts.

### 6.2 Two-ended retrieval

- **Forward from bodily description.** Queries begin with early anchors and related descriptions and passages they reveal: eg. "heeris weren whyte as whyte wolle and as snow," "eyes like fire", and radiance. Retrieved passages inform subsequent queries.

- **Backward from the later category.** Searches begin with passages containing *albino* and historical vocabulary that preceded or accompanied it, including "white negro", *leucaethiops*, Dondos, *kakerlak* and spelling variants. These searches are expanded through contextual vocabulary.

The two retrieval paths will be investigated separately before comparison, their overlap being of particular value.

### 6.3 Passage-level retrieval and annotation

Retrieval operates on overlapping 40-word windows with a 20-word hop. Each passage retains bibliographic provenance and location so that it can be inspected in context.

The annotation scheme distinguishes hair, skin, eyes, radiance and the functions ascribed to the eyes; the co-occurrence or independence of features; age cues and other explanations; the figure's status; genre and discourse; the presence and character of any recorded reaction; quotation, allusion or independent use; and confidence and interpretive uncertainty. The scheme will be piloted and revised before full annotation. Borderline examples will be retained rather than forced into a binary classification.

### 6.4 Models and baselines

The central methodological question is to what extent iterative semantic retrieval can discover relevant historical evidence that conventional lexical methods miss. Preliminary work has shown a 1972 clinical description of an "albino" child in Malta surfaces the description of Son of Man in the *English Apocalyptica* - documented here: https://github.com/leegee/mutatis-mutandis-passage-search

Evaluation will use the annotated gold set described in Section 6.7. Relevance will be assessed in historical context rather than inferred from semantic similarity alone. Precision and recall will be measured where the annotation permits, alongside the discovery of additional relevant evidence, the historical interpretability of results, retrieval-path validity, source provenance, reproducibility and computational cost. Plausible but misleading matches will be included in the assessment. Any data used for model fine-tuning will be separated from held-out evaluation data to avoid inflating performance.

The encoder under evaluation is MacBERTh (Manjavacas and Fonteyn 2022), a BERT-base model pre-trained on English from approximately 1450 to 1950. The current prototype derives contextual word representations from both token- and document-level processing. A detailed description is beyond the scope of this proposal (the code repository is listed in the appendix), but the above promising results come from processing text chunks of up to 510 subword tokens, with the resulting subword states are combined to obtain word-level representations. Each passage representation is the unweighted mean of the vectors for its 40 words, with a 20-word hop between successive windows. Passage vectors therefore retain information from wider document context rather than being calculated from isolated passages alone.

Where feasible the evaluation will compare the following approaches, using the same gold set and consistent relevance criteria:

1. **Lexical baseline.** BM25 and pattern matching over normalised and lemmatised text, supplemented by hand-built lexicons and historically informed variants.

2. **Off-the-shelf sentence embeddings.** A contemporary general-purpose sentence-embedding model applied to lightly normalised text.

3. **MacBERTh representations.** Layer-eight, final-layer and mean-of-last-four-layer vectors, with and without post-processing such as corpus-mean centring or whitening.

4. **Contrastively fine-tuned MacBERTh.** Fine-tuning for sentence similarity, with the approach selected during the pilot. Options include unsupervised contrastive learning or training on suitable pairs derived from the annotated sample, subject to held-out evaluation.

5. **Hybrid retrieval.** Lexical retrieval to secure candidate coverage, followed by dense or fine-tuned reranking.

The comparison will establish whether the additional complexity of contextual or hybrid retrieval is justified by improved discovery of relevant evidence. Post-processing and fine-tuning are experimental options, not assumed improvements.

Several important textual anchors, including Wycliffite renderings of Revelation 1:14, predate 1450 and fall outside MacBERTh's principal pre-training range, so the prototype's retrieval of a Middle English rendering is therefore a promising lead, but is not taken as evidence of reliable generalisation to earlier English. A pre-1450 subset of the gold set will be used to compare MacBERTh with the lexical baseline. Old and Middle English will remain primarily a matter of lexical and collocational search and close reading, rather than being treated as periods with equivalent model coverage. Early modern biblical translations of the same verses will provide in-range queries for comparison. Potential overlap between MacBERTh's pre-training data and the corpora searched will be documented.

Chronological filtering will be central to evaluating diachronic discovery. Time slicing is not currently envisaged as necessary for MacBERTh itself, given the breadth of its training period. If retrieval produces an unmanageable volume of results, clustering may organise candidates by genre or semantic field, without treating cluster membership as evidence of historical equivalence.

As a complementary experiment, token-level MacBERTh vectors may be stored for a small set of target words, including *white/whyte*, *hair/heer*, *wool/wolle* and *eye*. Clustering their contextual representations may help investigate variation in use and sense across contexts and periods. This experiment will remain subordinate to the main task of retrieving and interpreting passages.

Further extensions, including LLM-guided query expansion, orchestration frameworks or graph-based representations of passages and retrieval paths, will be considered only where they address a demonstrated limitation of simpler methods. Their contribution will be assessed empirically - none are a prerequisite for the core investigation.

### 6.6 Quotation and reception

Biblical and other identifiable quotations will not be discarded as noise but as evidence of reception and the continued availability of particular images. Passages will be annotated as quotation, allusion or independent use where this can be established. Results will distinguish searches that include quotations from those that exclude them, helping assess whether apparent continuity reflects repeated transmission, convention or independent description.

### 6.7 Validation

A hand-annotated sample will assess whether retrieval identifies passages relevant to the object. Precision will be examined by period and, where sample sizes permit, by feature and genre. Recall will be assessed using a gold set built from known cases: passages identified in White's thesis for 1650-1914, the anchor passages named in this outline, and passages found independently through close reading.

Where feasible, a second annotator will assess a subset, and the limits of agreement will be reported. False positives and borderline cases will be analysed as evidence about both the method and the definition of the object. The annotated sample is a research output in its own right.

### 6.8 Selection and the recording of reactions

The surviving record may preserve unusual encounters precisely because they provoked a response. The project will distinguish whether a text records a reaction to a described body from the kind of reaction recorded: astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust, or theological or medical interpretation. More mundane, matter-of-fact descriptions will receive particular attention as a counterpoint to symbolic or sensational accounts.

The frequency of recorded reactions can be measured in the surviving material, but the probability that an ordinary encounter was recorded at all cannot. The surviving texts must therefore not be treated as a representative sample of lived encounters.

## 7. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who himself has albinism. This informs the choice of questions and an awareness of the consequences of historical representation, but does not determine the meanings found in the evidence. The project does not assume that albinism or extraordinary paleness has an inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that writers, communities and traditions attached to visibly marked bodies. Evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own judgement will be kept distinct.

The principal output is a PhD thesis. Secondary outputs, subject to copyright and licensing, are an annotated passage collection, a reproducible retrieval pipeline and articles for digital humanities, literary and disability studies. Any release will document provenance, annotation decisions and limitations.

The research will be relevant to people with albinism and their families, educators, and organisations concerned with albinism and minority representation. By investigating a wider historical range of bodily descriptions, it will assess whether the surviving record challenges the predominantly negative current accounts of people with albinism.

Findings will be shared in accessible language, and the project will consider consulting organisations such as Standing Voice and national support groups on presentation.

## 9. Risks and limitations

- **Breadth.** The topic touches many traditions. Mitigation: the core, contextual and excluded strands in Section 4.5, a Year 1 review, and a comparative chapter that can be reduced.

- **Method performance.** MacBERTh may not outperform a lexical baseline for this object. Mitigation: comparative baselines and, if useful, a hybrid design; the thesis's claims will rest on close reading, with retrieval serving as support.

- **Sparse evidence.** The phenomenon may be rare or unevenly distributed. Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, cautious lexicon expansion and close reading where counts are low.

- **Corpus and OCR bias.** EEBO-TCP is hand-keyed, whereas ECCO and nineteenth-century material may contain OCR errors; spelling also varies. The pilot will test whether contextual representations help with orthographic variation and OCR noise rather than assuming that they do. Corpus coverage, source inspection and light normalisation will be documented, and any residual limitations reported.

- **An unstable object.** Age-related whiteness and the distinction between core and associated features may remain ambiguous. Mitigation: borderline categories and sensitivity tests of alternative inclusion decisions.

- **False continuity.** Similar descriptions may be mistaken for transmission. Mitigation: separate resemblance from documented reception, leaving unresolved relationships unresolved.

- **Circularity.** Seed descriptions shape what the system finds. Mitigation: two-ended retrieval, controls, analysis of false positives and validation through close reading.

- **Limits of the record.** Texts cannot show how representative their depictions were of ordinary encounters.

- **Language competence.** Old English, Latin and the comparative languages are read at different levels. The researcher has some competency in German, Scandinavian languages, Middle English and Old English, and is comfortable with specialist editions and linguistic analysis of Germanic languages. Specialist advice will be sought where necessary, particularly for Old Welsh and Old Irish.
