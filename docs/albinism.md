# From Theophany to Pathology: The Changing Semantics of Extreme Bodily Whiteness in Insular Literary Culture

*Preliminary research outline, revised draft. Items in \[square brackets\] need your input or verification.*

## 1. Summary

This project asks how a recurring cluster of bodily features, namely unusually white hair, very pale skin and unusual, pale or fiery eyes, acquired different symbolic meanings in the literary culture of the British Isles, and how those meanings interacted with the later emergence of "albinism" as a medical and racial category. It begins from the theophanic imagery of the "Son of Man" in Revelation 1 and the "Ancient of Days" in Daniel 7, follows the complex through medieval and early modern English, Welsh, Irish and Norse-influenced material, and ends with the nineteenth-century medicalisation of the albino body.

The project combines close literary-historical reading with a bounded computational method: diachronic semantic search that works from both ends of the timeline, with every machine-retrieved passage checked by hand. The computational work serves the humanities question to attempt to locate a rare image, and is not an end in itself.

## 2. Central question and hypothesis

**Question.** How did the symbolic meanings attached to extreme bodily whiteness change in insular literary culture between the medieval period and the nineteenth century, and under what conditions did a symbolic complex become attached to an actual bodily condition?

**Working hypothesis (to be tested, not assumed).** Extreme bodily whiteness carried unstable and contested meanings from early in its textual history, ranging from the numinous and blessed to the suspect and uncanny, and the later valence of pallor as cursed or unholy in Gothic and modern writing reflects a shift in those meanings. This is a hypothesis about a historically constructed category. The project does not assume that the cluster of features is a trans-historical symbol or a natural response to the phenotype.

## 3. Scope and corpora

### 3.1 Primary object

English literary, religious, natural-historical and medical writing from the medieval period to the nineteenth century, together with the biblical and classical material that demonstrably reached English writers. EEBO, ECCO, CLMET.

### 3.2 The insular framing

English alone is too thin a base, particularly for the earlier period, where native English mythological material is sparse. The project therefore takes the British Isles as its frame, adding Welsh and Irish material and Norse material in the form in which it entered English culture. The Welsh *gwyn* and the Irish *finn* are notable here: both cover whiteness, fairness, blessedness and holiness, so their semantic range is broader than "whiteness" alone. The project treats that breadth as part of the question, since it contrasts with the later Gothic and modern association of extreme pallor with the cursed and the unholy.

The non-English material is handled under two explicitly separate relations to the English corpus:

1. **Reception evidence.** Material with a documented channel into English culture within the period, such as nineteenth-century Eddic translation, antiquarianism and the medievalism of figures like William Morris, is treated as primary evidence for the English tradition.
2. **Comparative material.** Material without a demonstrable channel is used only to sharpen, by contrast or structural parallel, what is specific to the English case. No claim of descent or equivalence is made.

On Baldr: Snorri's pale Baldr is often argued to owe something to Christian imagery, and the earlier poetic Edda presents a less strongly pale figure under the same name which itself has connotations of brightness/extreme whiteness. Establishing independence from Christian sources is outside the project's scope. The image is nonetheless historically real and influential, and it is traced as part of the reception history. Baldr and the Ancient of Days are not presented as two independent witnesses to one complex.

Twentieth century reception lies outside the period but bears on how the project is framed. Norse whiteness-symbolism was appropriated in the twentieth century by racial-nationalist and occult movements (see Goodrick-Clarke on the occult roots of Nazism and Van Deusen on Old Norse reception history) \[confirm the specific works\]. This history is one reason the project treats the meanings of bodily whiteness as historically constructed, not natural or inherent. Its aim is to historicise such symbolism, and it does not endorse any use of it.

Material from classical ethnography and paradoxography (for example Pliny's and Solinus's accounts of the white-haired, pale-eyed people of Caucasian Albania) is included where it reached English readers, and treated as comparative otherwise.&#32;

### 3.3 Biblical and apocryphal sources

Revelation 1 and Daniel 7 supply the inherited vocabulary. 1 Enoch 106, which presents Noah's luminous, white-haired birth as a cause of paternal fear, offers an earlier and more ambivalent treatment, but was not available in English before Richard Laurence's 1821 translation of the Ethiopic text. Book of the Watchers material reached English readers earlier through the Greek fragments in Syncellus. \[Verify and cite the specific editions.\] Transmission facts of this kind constrain what can be claimed about what English writers could have known at a given date, and the project treats them as constraints.

## 4. Position within existing scholarship

Thomas White's 2012 Manchester thesis, *"Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours": A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650–1914*, establishes how albinism emerged as a medico-scientific category through travel writing, natural history, medical classification and racial discourse. The present project asks a different primary question: what is the longer history of the cultural semantics surrounding extreme bodily whiteness, and how did that older complex interact with, shape or get transformed by the later discourses White describes?

Charlotte Baker's work on the representation of albinism in Francophone and African contexts informs the project in two ways. It shows that the meaning of extreme bodily whiteness is produced locally within specific discursive and social conditions, which disciplines the project's own historical claims. And her grounding in critical disability studies offers a model for keeping embodiment, stigma and the lived consequences of visible difference in view even where the evidence is textual. For the Old English and Old Norse material the project draws on the work of Alaric Hall and Jackson Crawford on colour terminology and supernatural whiteness. \[Confirm the specific works you intend to cite.\]

## 5. Research questions

1. **Construction and change.** How is extreme bodily whiteness symbolically constructed in insular literary culture from the medieval period to the nineteenth century, and how do its semantic associations, including valence (blessed or holy versus cursed or unholy), change across that period?
2. **Discursive interaction and medicalisation.** How do religious, literary, medical and scientific discourses interact in shaping representations of visibly marked bodies, and under what conditions do symbolic representations become attached to actual bodily conditions such as albinism?
3. **Selection, salience and the mundane phenotype.** Textual survival favours encounters that provoked reaction. Does the corpus preserve evidence of matter-of-fact encounters with the phenotype, and how do such depictions compare with symbolic ones? (See the methodology note.)
4. **Continuity and comparison.** What continuities and discontinuities can be identified between earlier theophanic and literary representations and later medicalised understandings, and what do the Welsh, Irish and Norse materials show about what is distinctive in the English case?
5. **Method.** Can a two-ended diachronic semantic search, anchored on physical description at one end and the modern term at the other, retrieve recurring physiognomically similar types without presupposing their equivalence?

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Overall design

Computational retrieval identifies candidate passages across the corpus at scale. Close reading then interprets them. The aim is to find large-scale patterns in symbolic association that conventional reading cannot easily survey, and to subject those patterns to detailed literary and historical analysis.

### 6.2 Lexicon-chain search from both ends

A seed lexicon is built from two directions:

- **Forward from the earliest material.** Descriptive anchors drawn from Revelation 1, Daniel 7 and comparable early passages, namely white hair ("like wool", "like snow"), pale skin and pale, flame-like or red eyes. The lexicon is expanded slice by slice through collocates and nearest neighbours.
- **Backward from the modern term.** "Albino" and its historical variants and neighbours (to be compiled from White's thesis and from corpus evidence), expanded backward in the same way.

The test is whether the two chains meet, and where. Anchoring on description rather than on the modern term reduces anachronism, but the category of "albino-like" is the project's own construction and is treated as such.

### 6.3 Passage-level retrieval

Because the features are usually spread across clauses, retrieval works at sentence or short-passage level, with each feature (hair, skin, eyes) annotated separately. A prototype has already retrieved relevant Welsh mythological material from the query "hair as white as wool". \[Add any results you want to cite.\]

### 6.4 Models and time slices

- A historical language model (MacBERTh, whose coverage is roughly 1450 to 1950 \[confirm\]) is used for the earlier and middle periods, and a modern model for the late period.
- **No cross-model alignment.** The same queries are run in each model separately, and the project compares the passages retrieved and their neighbours, not the vectors. This is more transparent and avoids the noise of aligning contextual embeddings.
- Time slices are sized by corpus volume, with overlapping windows and genre balancing, since the phenomenon is rare and religious texts would otherwise dominate the early slices.
- Material before the models' coverage, and the Welsh, Irish and Old Norse material for which comparable models are not available \[confirm\], is analysed by lexical and collocational methods on annotated editions.

### 6.5 Quotation and reception

Biblical quotation is not filtered out. Each occurrence is tagged as quotation, allusion or independent use, and quotation is treated as reception data in its own right. Statistical summaries report results with and without quotations.

### 6.6 Controls

To show that the complex is distinct from a generic association between whiteness and holiness, results are compared with other colour and bodily-extreme terms.

### 6.7 Validation

A hand-annotated sample of retrieved passages, double-annotated where possible, checks the retrieval against close reading, and gives precision estimates for each period. Disagreements are reported. The annotated sample is a research output in its own right.

### 6.8 Methodology note: the recording of reaction

The surviving record may preserve reactions to the phenotype rather than ordinary encounters with it. Each occurrence is therefore annotated on two dimensions: whether the text records an explicit reaction at all, and, where it does, its character and intensity (astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust, theological or medical interpretation). Special attention goes to matter-of-fact descriptions, which are the best test of the selection effect. The frequency of reaction in the surviving record can be measured directly. The probability that an unremarkable encounter was recorded at all can only be approached indirectly, and the project keeps these two questions apart when interpreting counts.

## 7. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who himself has albinism. This informs the choice of questions and brings an awareness of the consequences of historical representation. It does not determine the meanings the research identifies. The project does not assume that albinism or extreme bodily whiteness has inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that particular writers, communities and traditions have attached to visibly marked bodies. The methodology is explicitly reflexive, with interpretative claims grounded in textual, linguistic and historical evidence, and with evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own judgement kept distinct.

### 8.1 Beneficiaries and impact

This project examines how extreme bodily whiteness, including the features now associated with albinism, has been represented in the literary culture of the British Isles. Existing scholarship points mainly to representations of monstrosity, wonder and medical curiosity. The project asks a different question: what range of representations survives in the record, including veneration and matter-of-fact treatment, and what can the record tell us about ordinary lives?

The main beneficiaries beyond academia are people with albinism and their families. Albinism affects roughly 1 in 17,000 people in Europe and North America [confirm and cite], yet the cultural history available to them is largely one of stigma. A fuller historical picture, including positive images, could support education and public understanding.

The project will release an openly accessible, annotated collection of the passages it identifies, together with a plain-language summary of its findings. It will seek to consult albinism organisations, such as Standing Voice and national support groups, on how results are presented, and it will share findings with them.

## 9. Risks

- **Sparse data.** The phenomenon is rare. Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, seed-lexicon expansion, and hand annotation in place of statistical claims where counts are low.
- **Model coverage and noise.** OCR errors and spelling variation affect early printed text. Mitigation: use of an EEBO/ECCO-trained model, MacBERTh, and minimal normalisation.
- **Scope.** The insular frame is broad. Mitigation: the English corpus is the primary object, and the Welsh, Irish and Norse material is clearly separated into reception and comparative tracks.
- **Circularity.** Anchors shape results. Mitigation: two-ended search, controls and validation against close reading.
