# Marked Whiteness: Hair, Skin and Eyes in English Writing from the Old English Period to the Nineteenth Century

*Preliminary research outline, third revised draft. The title remains provisional. Items in square brackets require verification or a decision.*

## 1. Starting point and contribution

Thomas White's thesis, *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650–1914* (University of Manchester, 2011), provides an essential starting point. White traces the changing cultural and scientific significance of albinism through travel writing, encounters with unusually white people, natural history, medical classification, biological research and the self-representation of people with albinism. His work demonstrates that albinism was not simply a timeless medical condition awaiting scientific discovery: its meanings and social consequences developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity.

The present project does not seek to repeat that history. Its principal object is different: **the representation and interpretation of extraordinary bodily whiteness in English writing over a longer period, extending from Old English literature to the nineteenth century**. It investigates descriptions of unusually white hair, very pale skin and distinctive eyes, including their appearance in religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical and medical contexts. It asks what meanings these descriptions acquired, how those meanings changed, and whether specific historical connections can be demonstrated between earlier representations and the later category of albinism.

White's work is relevant both as scholarship to build on and as a boundary against which to define the proposed research. His discussion of earlier attempts to identify historical figures as albinos does not amount to a systematic investigation of the longer literary history of extraordinary bodily whiteness. The proposed project would investigate that history in its own right, while drawing on White's account when the earlier material encounters the period and discourses he studies.

The project therefore shifts the emphasis from the history of a category to the history of a cluster of bodily descriptions and their cultural meanings. It does not presume that these descriptions constitute an early form of albinism, that they belong to a single tradition, or that their meanings culminate in the modern medical category. Establishing whether any such relationships existed, and on what evidence they can be claimed, is part of the research.

## 2. Summary and central question

The project asks how a recurring cluster of bodily features—unusually white hair, very pale skin, distinctive pale or fiery eyes, and occasionally bodily radiance—was described and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to the nineteenth century.

Its principal research question is:

**How was extraordinary bodily whiteness represented and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to the nineteenth century, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism?**

The project combines literary-historical research and close reading with a bounded computational method: passage-level semantic retrieval across historical corpora. Retrieval begins both with descriptions of bodily features in early material and with the later terminology of albinism. The resulting passages are examined in their textual, linguistic and historical contexts. The computational method is a means of finding evidence that conventional reading may miss, not an independent research objective.

The working hypothesis is that extraordinary bodily whiteness has acquired multiple meanings, potentially including the numinous, the wondrous, the ominous, the monstrous and the medical, whose availability and significance vary by period and genre. These meanings need not form a continuous tradition, and apparently similar descriptions need not have the same significance.

The project remains open to findings that complicate or disprove this hypothesis. Earlier and later representations may prove largely unrelated; matter-of-fact descriptions may be scarce; and some apparent patterns may be attributable to conventional imagery, textual transmission or the survival of particular kinds of writing.

## 3. Research questions

The project addresses four related humanities questions.

1. **Representation and historical change.** How is extraordinary bodily whiteness described in English writing, and what meanings are attached to it across periods and genres? How do religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical and medical interpretations compare, including differences in moral or emotional valence?

2. **The body and its interpretation.** How do writers distinguish, or conflate, unusual hair, skin, eyes and bodily radiance? When are these features presented as signs of holiness, wonder, danger, monstrosity, physical difference or a condition requiring explanation? What evidence survives of descriptions that do not provoke an explicit reaction?

3. **Historical relationship and discontinuity.** Where can specific connections be demonstrated between earlier representations and later accounts of albinism? What roles do quotation, allusion, translation, shared authorities and genre conventions play? Where does the evidence indicate discontinuity, independent development or an unresolved relationship?

4. **The English tradition in context.** What do biblical, classical and non-English sources contribute to the English evidence when there is a demonstrable channel of reception? Where comparison is useful without such a channel, what can it clarify about the specificity of English representations?

The computational question is subordinate to these: can passage-level semantic retrieval help identify relevant descriptions without assuming that resemblance establishes equivalence or historical transmission?

## 4. Object of study and scope

### 4.1 Defining extraordinary bodily whiteness

“Extraordinary bodily whiteness” is an analytical term developed for this project, not a historical category assumed to have been recognised consistently across the period. It encompasses several distinct features whose relationships must be established through the evidence.

The core object comprises descriptions of human or human-like bodies, including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures, in which:

- white or very pale hair occurs alongside at least one further marked bodily feature, such as unusually pale skin or distinctive pale, red or flame-like eyes; or
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a bodily peculiarity in its own right.

Associated features will be recorded separately: bodily luminosity or radiance; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; and the reactions of observers. These may illuminate the interpretation of a description but do not, by themselves, establish that it belongs to the core object.

The project excludes generic symbolism of white as purity or holiness when it is not attached to a described body, similes involving snow or wool applied to non-bodies, and conventional descriptions of fair complexion. Borderline cases will be retained in separate categories where excluding them would obscure a potentially significant relationship.

### 4.2 White hair, age and markedness

White hair is frequently associated with age. Daniel 7's Ancient of Days is a particularly important case because the imagery of white hair and great age belongs to a figure whose majesty and antiquity are central to the representation. The project must distinguish this from descriptions in which whiteness marks a body in some other way.

Each relevant passage will therefore be annotated for explicit or implicit age cues, explicit youth, and the absence of a discernible age cue. Strong age-related cases will be retained as a separate analytical group rather than automatically treated as evidence of the core phenomenon.

Revelation 1:14 applies to the Son of Man imagery resembling the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9, including hair white like wool. The project will examine how this transfer was interpreted in the Latin exegetical tradition, beginning with Bede's Explanatio Apocalypsis and comparing it with earlier commentary on Daniel, particularly Jerome, and other relevant medieval commentators. Particular attention will be paid to explanations of whiteness in terms of divine majesty, eternity and related attributes, and to how these interpretations were transmitted into English biblical and literary writing. The aim is to establish how the imagery was understood in particular historical contexts, rather than to assume a continuous tradition or a connection with descriptions of bodily difference.

Daniel 7 and Revelation 1 thus provide important textual and early reception anchors, but neither is assumed in advance to be an instance of the same phenomenon as the descriptions of bodily difference that motivate the project.

### 4.3 Controls

The project will use a small set of controls to test whether apparent patterns are specific to extraordinary whiteness or reflect more general literary conventions. These will include other hair and skin colours, such as red, black and yellow; other bodily extremes, including giants, dwarfs and the very old; and ordinary descriptions of white or grey hair, including age-related hair and snow-covered beards.

The final control set will be determined by the pilot study. Its purpose is interpretive as well as quantitative: to help distinguish marked bodily descriptions from conventional imagery and to identify which comparisons genuinely illuminate the core object.

### 4.4 English writing and historical periods

The primary corpus comprises English literary, religious, natural-historical and medical writing from the Old English period to the nineteenth century. Biblical and classical sources are included insofar as their transmission into English writing can be established.

The work is divided into three tiers:

1. **Old English, approximately 700–1150.** A bounded investigation using lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading of the original texts. Modern translations and other finding aids may help locate candidates but will not be treated as evidence of the Old English text's meaning.

2. **Approximately 1450–1900.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus, drawing on EEBO, ECCO and CLMET. Passage-level semantic retrieval will support the analysis of early modern and later representations.

3. **Middle English, approximately 1150–1450.** Targeted lexical searches and selected close readings will bridge the other periods, without a claim to systematic computational coverage. [Specify the resources and assess the scale of this component during the pilot.]

The main weight of the project falls on the early modern and later material, where the computational approach can be tested at scale. The earlier work provides a bounded investigation of selected textual traditions rather than a comprehensive survey of all medieval writing.

### 4.5 Biblical, apocryphal, classical and vernacular traditions

Daniel 7 and Revelation 1 provide central examples of extraordinary white hair in religious representation. In Daniel 7:9, the Ancient of Days has hair like pure wool; in Revelation 1:13–14, comparable imagery is applied to the Son of Man figure. The *Similitudes of Enoch* (1 Enoch 37–71), particularly chapter 46, offer a Jewish apocalyptic comparison in which the white-haired Head of Days and the Son of Man remain distinct figures. The account of Noah’s birth in 1 Enoch 106 presents a different and more ambivalent treatment of extraordinary whiteness: Noah’s unusual appearance,   including its luminous quality, provokes paternal fear and questions about his parentage. These texts provide contrasting contexts in which extraordinary appearance may signify heavenly authority, exceptional status or disturbing difference. Their relationships will be examined without presupposing direct literary influence.

The transmission history of biblical and apocryphal material will be central to the analysis. The availability of particular texts, translations and commentaries to English writers varied by period and will be established where possible. The project will distinguish the survival of an earlier text from evidence of its reception or influence in later writing.

Welsh Arthurian and related vernacular traditions provide another important field of investigation. The analysis will begin with Olwen, Gwyn ap Nudd, the Very White Witch and the Black Witch, and other potentially relevant figures in *Culhwch ac Olwen*, alongside material from *Pwyll* and *Manawydan*. It will investigate the meanings and narrative functions of *gwyn/gwen*—terms whose semantic range includes whiteness, fairness and brightness—and whether their use is associated disproportionately with beauty, heroism, supernatural status, Otherworldliness or liminality. These associations will be established through textual analysis rather than inferred from names alone; particular attention will be paid to whether descriptions of hair, skin and eyes occur together.

The project will not assume that Christianity created these associations. Instead, it will investigate possible interactions between inherited Welsh vocabulary and literary conventions, Christian symbolism, and the transmission and transformation of vernacular narrative. Irish *fionn/find* material may provide a controlled comparison where it illuminates a specific question. Old English and Old Norse traditions will be investigated independently rather than treated as interchangeable evidence for a common northern European symbolism.

Where the evidence permits, the comparative analysis will consider whether recurring associations are best explained by inherited linguistic or cultural patterns, borrowing, Christian convergence, independent development or some combination of these processes. Claims about common Indo-European inheritance or historical transmission will require evidence beyond resemblance in vocabulary or physical description.

Classical ethnography and paradoxography, including accounts by Pliny and Solinus of unusually white-haired or pale-eyed peoples associated with Caucasian Albania, will be considered where their reception in English writing can be demonstrated. Otherwise, such material will serve as comparative context rather than evidence of influence.

Throughout, the project will distinguish shared bodily description from demonstrable historical connection. A source will be treated as evidence for a literary tradition at a particular date only where its textual context, availability, reception or mediation can be established.

### 4.6 Non-English material: reception and comparison

Welsh, Irish and Norse material is not part of the primary corpus. It will be used in two distinct ways.

**Reception evidence.** Where a text demonstrably reached English readers through translation, antiquarian scholarship or literary adaptation, the English version or reception is evidence for the English tradition. This includes relevant nineteenth-century Eddic translations and medievalist writing, including the reception of Norse material by figures such as William Morris. Translated vocabulary is evidence of the choices made by translators and the imagery made available to English readers; it cannot, by itself, establish the meaning of the source tradition.

**Comparative material.** Where no channel of transmission can be established, non-English material may be considered to clarify a specific interpretive problem raised by the English evidence. Such comparisons will not imply descent, equivalence or a shared origin.

The semantic ranges of Welsh *gwyn* and Irish *finn*, for example, may provide a useful contrast for examining how whiteness, fairness and related qualities are expressed in different linguistic and cultural settings. Their relevance will depend on the particular textual evidence, rather than an assumption that their semantic ranges correspond directly to English meanings.

Baldr's pallor in Snorri's Edda raises a related question. Its relationship to Christian imagery has been debated, and the earlier poetic material presents a different configuration of the figure. The project will trace relevant English reception where evidence supports it, without treating Baldr and the Ancient of Days as independent witnesses to one common tradition or attempting to resolve the origins of the Norse image.

### 4.7 Later appropriation

Twentieth-century racial-nationalist and occult appropriations of Norse imagery fall outside the principal period of study. They may be relevant to the reception history and to explaining why the meanings of whiteness should be historicised rather than treated as inherent. ## 5. Existing scholarship and contribution

White's thesis provides the principal point of departure for the history of albinism as a category and for the relationship between scientific accounts and the social identities of people with albinism. Its coverage of travel encounters, medical thought, biological research and life writing is indispensable to the proposed project's later material. The present research will use White to identify relevant sources and debates, while avoiding a second general history of the same category over substantially the same period.

The difference is one of emphasis and research object, not a claim that White ignores cultural representation. The proposed study begins with a broader and earlier set of bodily descriptions and asks how their meanings developed within English writing, whether or not those descriptions were ever associated with albinism. It then investigates the extent to which the later medical category intersected with this longer cultural history.

Charlotte Baker's work on representations of albinism in Francophone and African contexts offers a further point of reference. It demonstrates the importance of examining representations within particular social and discursive settings, rather than assuming a universal meaning for visible bodily difference. Her engagement with critical disability studies is also relevant to the project's treatment of stigma, embodiment and the consequences of representation.

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Literary-historical design

The project combines corpus-scale retrieval with close reading. Computational methods will identify candidate passages and help reveal patterns that are difficult to survey through conventional reading alone. Interpretation will then consider each passage's wording, genre, authorship where known, textual context, historical setting and relationship to other sources.

The project will not treat embedding similarity, keyword frequency or cluster membership as self-sufficient evidence of meaning. Quantitative patterns will guide investigation; literary-historical evidence will determine what can be claimed.

### 6.2 Two-ended semantic retrieval

The retrieval process begins from two directions.

**Forward from bodily description.** Initial queries are drawn from early textual anchors and related descriptions: white hair like wool or snow, very pale skin, pale or fiery eyes, and associated forms of radiance. Candidate passages are examined, and their relevant vocabulary can inform subsequent queries.

**Backward from the later category.** Searches begin with *albino*, its historical spelling variants and related terminology. These searches identify relevant passages in the later corpus and can be expanded through contextual vocabulary and neighbouring passages.

The two chains are investigated separately before their results are compared. Their overlap identifies places for close reading; it does not establish that the two bodies of material are historically connected.

A claim of connection requires evidence appropriate to the case, such as identifiable quotation, allusion, translation, a shared authority or a demonstrable genre convention. Where the evidence establishes resemblance but no channel of transmission, the relationship remains unproven. Where the available evidence cannot decide the matter, the conclusion will remain open.

### 6.3 Passage-level retrieval and annotation

Retrieval will operate at sentence or short-passage level because the relevant features may be distributed across several clauses. Each candidate passage will retain its bibliographic provenance and location within the source.

The annotation scheme will distinguish:

- hair, skin, eyes and radiance;
- whether features occur together or independently;
- age cues and other explanations for the appearance;
- the figure's status, where identifiable;
- genre and discourse;
- the presence and character of any recorded reaction;
- quotation, allusion or independent use;
- confidence in inclusion and relevant interpretive uncertainties.

The scheme will be piloted and revised before full annotation. Borderline examples will be retained where useful, rather than forced into a binary classification.

### 6.6 Quotation and reception

Biblical and other identifiable quotations will not be discarded as duplicates or noise. They are evidence of reception and of the continued availability of particular images. Passages will be annotated for quotation, allusion or independent use where this can be established.

Summaries of patterns will distinguish results that include quotations from results that exclude them. This will help assess whether apparent continuity reflects repeated textual transmission, broader conventions or independent descriptions.

### 6.7 Validation

A hand-annotated sample will assess whether the retrieval process identifies passages relevant to the project's object. Where feasible, a second annotator will independently assess a subset, allowing disagreements and the limits of agreement measures to be reported.

Precision will be examined by period and, where sample sizes allow, by feature or genre. False positives and borderline cases will be analysed as evidence about the method and the definition of the object, not merely discarded. The annotated sample will be a research output in its own right.

### 6.8 Selection and the recording of reactions

The surviving record may preserve unusual encounters precisely because they provoked a response. The project will therefore distinguish two questions: whether a text records a reaction to a described body, and what kind of reaction it records.

Where a reaction is present, it will be annotated for its character, such as astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust or theological or medical interpretation. Matter-of-fact descriptions will receive particular attention because they provide an important counterpoint to explicitly symbolic or sensational accounts.

The frequency of recorded reactions can be measured within the retrieved material. The probability that an ordinary encounter was recorded at all cannot be measured directly from that material. The project will keep these questions distinct and avoid treating the surviving textual record as a representative sample of lived encounters.

## 7. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who has albinism. This informs the choice of questions and brings an awareness of the consequences of historical representation, but it does not determine the meanings identified in the evidence.

The project does not assume that albinism or extraordinary bodily whiteness has an inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that particular writers, communities and traditions attached to visibly marked bodies. Interpretive claims will be grounded in textual, linguistic and historical evidence, with evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own judgement kept distinct.

## 8. Outputs, beneficiaries and three-year plan

### 8.1 Expected outputs

The principal output will be a PhD thesis presenting a literary-historical account of extraordinary bodily whiteness in English writing and assessing the extent to which its representations can be related to the later category of albinism.

Subject to copyright, licensing and source restrictions, the project will also produce an annotated collection of passages, a reproducible retrieval pipeline and research outputs for digital humanities and literary or disability studies. The public release will document provenance, annotation decisions and limitations so that the collection can be critically reused.

### 8.2 Beneficiaries and impact

The principal contribution is scholarly. Beyond academia, the research may be relevant to people with albinism and their families, educators, and organisations concerned with albinism and disability representation.

The project will investigate whether the historical record contains a wider range of representations than those most familiar in modern accounts, including positive, ambiguous, contradictory and matter-of-fact examples. It will not assume in advance that a positive historical inheritance exists or that literary depictions correspond to the experiences of people with albinism.

The project will seek to share its findings in accessible language and consult relevant organisations, such as Standing Voice and national support groups, on how results are presented. ## 9. Risks and limitations

- **Sparse evidence.** The phenomenon may be rare or unevenly distributed. The project will use passage-level retrieval, expand its lexicons cautiously and rely on close reading rather than statistical generalisation where counts are low.

- **Corpus and OCR bias.** Digitised texts, spelling variation and OCR errors may distort retrieval. The project will document corpus coverage, inspect source texts and use minimal normalisation where possible.

- **Uneven chronological coverage.** Computational retrieval is feasible for only part of the period. Old English work will be bounded, Middle English targeted, and the principal computational analysis concentrated on the early modern and later corpora.

- **An unstable object of study.** Age-related whiteness and the distinction between core and associated features may remain ambiguous. The pilot will refine the working definition, retain borderline categories and test the effect of alternative inclusion decisions.

- **False historical continuity.** Similar descriptions may be mistaken for evidence of transmission. The project will distinguish semantic resemblance from documented reception and leave unresolved relationships unresolved.

- **Circularity in retrieval.** The seed descriptions shape what the system can find. Two-ended retrieval, controls, examination of false positives and validation against close reading will help expose this limitation rather than eliminate it by assertion.

- **Limits of the surviving record.** Literary and medical texts cannot establish how representative their depictions were of ordinary encounters. The project will distinguish what can be said about textual representation from what remains unknown about lived experience.
