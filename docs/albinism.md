# Representing Visible Difference: Extraordinary Paleness of Hair, Skin and Eyes in English Writing, c. 700-1914

*Preliminary research outline, fourth revised draft. The title remains provisional. Items in square brackets require verification or a decision.*

## 1. Starting point and contribution

Thomas White's thesis, *Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours: A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650-1914* (University of Manchester, 2011), is the essential starting point. White traces the changing significance of albinism through travel writing, natural history, medical classification, biological research and the self-representation of people with albinism. He shows that albinism was not a timeless condition awaiting scientific discovery, but a category whose meanings and social consequences developed through historically specific discourses of human difference, race, pathology and identity.

This project does not repeat that work. It studies how unusually pale hair, skin and eyes were described and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to the seventeenth century, and compares that evidence with the later period White covers. It asks what meanings these descriptions acquired in religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical and medical contexts, how those meanings changed, and whether specific historical connections can be demonstrated between earlier representations and the later category of albinism.

White's discussion of earlier attempts to identify historical figures as albinos is not a systematic study of the longer literary history of such descriptions; that history is this project's subject. The emphasis shifts from the history of a category to the history of a cluster of bodily descriptions. The project does not presume that these descriptions are an early form of a medical condition, that they belong to a single tradition, or that their meanings culminate in modern albinism. Whether any such relationship exists, and on what evidence it can be claimed, is part of the research.

The project is framed within the study of how visible bodily difference has been represented. It draws on literary history and disability studies, and attends to stigma, embodiment and the consequences of representation, without claiming that descriptions it examines concern disabled or medicalised bodies.

## 2. Summary, central question and hypotheses

The project asks how a recurring cluster of bodily features (unusually white hair, very pale skin, distinctive pale or fiery eyes, and occasionally bodily radiance) was described and interpreted in English writing across more than a millennium. Its central question is:

**How was extraordinary paleness of the body represented and interpreted in English writing from the Old English period to the nineteenth century, and what historical relationships, if any, can be demonstrated between these representations and the later medical category of albinism?**

The project combines close reading with a bounded computational method: passage-level semantic retrieval over large historical corpora, started from two directions (early bodily descriptions such as "heeris weren whyte as whyte wolle and as snow," and the later vocabulary of albinism). Retrieved passages are examined in their textual, linguistic and historical contexts. Retrieval is a means of finding evidence that conventional reading may miss, not an independent research objective.

Two working hypotheses are stated so that they can fail.

- **H1 (period-specific meanings).** Before about 1550, English descriptions of extraordinarily pale bodies attach mainly to the numinous, otherworldly or wondrous, and carry no naturalistic explanation of the body as a human variation. Natural-historical and medical explanation of such bodies emerges in English chiefly in the seventeenth century, with travel writing and natural history. H1 fails if matter-of-fact or naturalistic accounts are found substantially earlier, or if numinous framings persist unchanged into the medical period.
- **H2 (limited reception).** Later naturalistic accounts draw on classical, travel and medical authorities rather than on the earlier religious and literary imagery. H2 fails if chains of quotation, allusion or translation link the two bodies of material. Combinations of both will be noted.

The project welcomes findings that complicate or disprove either hypothesis. Earlier and later representations may prove largely unrelated; matter-of-fact descriptions may be scarce; apparent patterns may be attributable to conventional imagery, textual transmission or the accidents of survival.

## 3. Research questions

1. **Representation and historical change.** How is extraordinary paleness described in English writing, and what meanings attach to it across periods and genres (religious, literary, supernatural, natural-historical, medical), including differences of moral or emotional valence?
2. **The body and its interpretation.** How do writers distinguish or conflate unusual hair, skin, eyes, bodily radiance and the function of the eyes (for example, claims about sight)? When are these features signs of holiness, wonder, danger, monstrosity, physical difference or a condition requiring explanation? What survives of descriptions that provoke no explicit reaction?
3. **Historical relationship and discontinuity.** Where can specific connections be demonstrated between earlier representations and later accounts of "albinism"? What roles do quotation, allusion, translation, shared authorities and genre conventions play? Where does the evidence indicate discontinuity or an unresolved relationship?
4. **The English tradition in context.** What do biblical, classical and other sources contribute where a demonstrable channel of reception exists, and what can bounded comparison clarify about the specificity of English representations?

The computational question is subordinate: can passage-level semantic retrieval help identify relevant descriptions without assuming that resemblance establishes equivalence or transmission?

## 4. Object of study and scope

### 4.1 Defining extraordinary paleness, and a note on terminology

"Extraordinary paleness" is an analytical term developed for this project, not a historical category assumed to have been recognised consistently. It is used in preference to "whiteness" to avoid confusion with the racial sense of that word in modern scholarship, and in preference to "albinism" to avoid presupposing the medical category. Historical sources' own terms ("white", "hoary", "wan", and others) are reported in quotation and analysed in context. Outside quotation the project uses "person with albinism" and avoids "albino" as a noun. Some historical terms in the later corpus (for example "white negro" and "kakerlak") are racialised or offensive; they are evidence to be analysed and will be flagged as such in any output.

The core object comprises descriptions of human or human-like bodies, including divine, angelic, supernatural and monstrous figures, in which:

- white or very pale hair occurs alongside at least one further marked bodily feature, such as unusually pale skin or distinctive pale, red or flame-like eyes; or
- white or very pale hair, or very pale skin, is presented as a bodily peculiarity in its own right.

Associated features are recorded separately: bodily luminosity; fiery or piercing eyes without accompanying pallor (for example Grendel's eyes); the function ascribed to the eyes, such as better sight at night; whiteness of clothing or surroundings; and the reactions of observers. These may illuminate a description without establishing that it belongs to the core object.

Excluded are generic symbolism of white (for example as purity or holiness) not attached to a described body, similes of snow or wool applied to non-bodies, and descriptions of conventional fair complexion. Borderline cases are kept in separate categories where excluding them would obscure a potentially significant relationship.

**Known confounds.**

"White-headed", "towhead", "flaxen" and similar words often describe fair-haired children and will pollute retrieval. Lexical work will draw on Biggam's studies of Old English colour terms, the *Thesaurus of Old English* and the *Historical Thesaurus of English*, as well as the clustering of semantic embeddings.

### 4.2 White hair, age and markedness

White hair may be associated with age, wisdom, purity, radiance and divine authority, but these associations are not interchangeable or self-evident. Patristic interpretations of Daniel 7:9 illustrate their plurality. Jerome distinguishes the symbolism of the Ancient of Days' apparent age from that of his wool-like hair: he interprets the figure's age as signifying the maturity of divine judgement, and the comparison of the hair to pure wool as signifying the purity and impartiality of that judgement (*Commentarii in Danielem*, on Daniel 7:9; translated by Gleason L. Archer, 2024).

Origen, in *Homilies on Numbers* 27.12.5, discusses Libnah, whose name he interprets as "whitening", and connects the Ancient of Days' wool-like hair with the splendour of the true light and the brightness of heavenly visions (translated by Thomas P. Scheck, 2009). Cyril of Jerusalem, in *Catechetical Lecture* 15.21, explains that the Father's white clothing and wool-like hair are described "after the manner of men". He connects the imagery with Isaiah 1:18, in which sins are made white as snow and wool, interpreting it in terms of forgiveness and sinlessness. Bede's *Expositio Apocalypseos* (Book I, chapter 3), commenting on Revelation 1:14, interprets the whiteness of the head as signifying "the antiquity and immortality of majesty" (*antiquitas et immortalitas maiestatis*). He also interprets the wool and snow imagery in relation to the righteous and the innumerable multitude of the elect.

These readings demonstrate that Christian interpreters assigned different, sometimes overlapping theological meanings to the imagery. Their significance for English writing must be established through evidence of transmission and reception, rather than presumed from the existence of the commentaries.

Daniel 7:9 and Revelation 1:13-14 provide important textual and reception-historical points of reference. The analysis will begin with the Aramaic wording of Daniel 7:9, examining the description of the Ancient of Days, the imagery of white clothing and wool-like hair, and the relationship between these features and the surrounding vision of divine judgement and sovereignty. It will investigate the language and imagery in their ancient literary and cultural contexts, including relevant parallels elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible and ancient Near Eastern traditions, distinguishing established conventions from features that may be distinctive to Daniel.

The project will then examine the Greek versions of Daniel and the reuse of the imagery in Revelation 1:13-14, asking what was preserved, reinterpreted or potentially altered in transmission. The subsequent Latin exegetical tradition will be explored through selected commentaries, beginning with Bede's *Expositio Apocalypseos* and comparing relevant interpretations of Daniel and Revelation. The analysis will trace demonstrable transmission into English biblical and literary writing, distinguishing the availability of a text or interpretation from evidence of its reception.

These passages are not assumed to represent the same phenomenon as the descriptions of bodily difference that motivate the project. Their importance lies in the opportunity they offer to investigate how extraordinary whiteness acquired, retained and changed its meanings across textual, linguistic and interpretive contexts.

Each relevant passage will be annotated for explicit or implicit age cues, explicit youth, and the absence of a discernible age cue. Strongly age-related cases will form a separate analytical group rather than automatically being treated as evidence of the core phenomenon. Cases of whiteness without an evident age association, including the reported premature whitening of Fionn's hair in Irish tradition [source and circumstances to be verified], are of particular interest.

### 4.3 Controls

Controls test whether apparent patterns are specific to extraordinary paleness or reflect general literary convention: other hair and skin colours (red, black, yellow, green); other bodily extremes (giants, dwarves, the very old); and ordinary descriptions of white, hoary and grey hair, including snow-covered beards. The final set will be determined by the pilot. Its purpose is interpretive as well as quantitative.

### 4.4 Corpus and periods

The primary corpus is English writing from the Old English period to the nineteenth century, in several tiers.

1. **Old English, c. 700-1150.** Lexical and collocational searches in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus, followed by close reading of the original texts. Starting points include *Wonders of the East* and the wider marvellous-peoples tradition, Grendel's eyes, and *ælfscȳne* and related vocabulary (Hall). Translations may help locate candidates but are not evidence of Old English meaning.
2. **Middle English, c. 1150-1450.** Targeted lexical searches and selected close readings, without a claim to systematic computational coverage. The focus is the channels through which earlier authorities reached English readers: Wycliffite and other biblical translations, Isidore \[check his treatment of the Albani\], Higden's *Polychronicon* and Trevisa's translation, Bartholomaeus Anglicus in Trevisa's version, and Mandeville. \[Specify resources and assess scale during the pilot.\]
3. **c. 1450-1650.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus: EEBO-TCP.
4. **c. 1650-1914.** The principal computational and interpretive corpus: ECCO and a nineteenth-century source to be chosen in the pilot \[candidates: HathiTrust, Gale's nineteenth-century collections, Wellcome Collection texts, the *Philosophical Transactions*\]. CLMET may serve as a small test set but is too small to carry the nineteenth century.

The weight of interpretive effort falls on the Old English and Middle English material and on the early modern period before 1650. White's period (c. 1650-1914) functions as a comparator and as a large-scale test of the method, using his findings as a source of known passages (see 6.7).

### 4.5 What is core, what is contextual, and what is excluded

The project is deliberately bounded. A three-year doctorate cannot provide complete coverage of all the traditions that touch this topic, and the design treats breadth as a risk to be managed, not a virtue.

**Core (committed).** Old English close reading (4.4, tier 1); Middle English channel studies (tier 2); early modern retrieval and close reading before 1650; the backward search from the later vocabulary of albinism; and the assessment of reception and discontinuity (H1, H2).

**Contextual (bounded, in service of the core).**

- *Biblical and exegetical reception.* Daniel 7 and Revelation 1 and one case study of Bede (4.2), as a section of the Old English or Middle English work. Apocryphal comparison is limited to the *Similitudes of Enoch* (1 Enoch 46) and the birth of Noah (1 Enoch 106), where Noah's luminous, unusual body provokes paternal fear; their reception is traced only through demonstrable channels such as Richard Laurence's English translation of 1821 and later revisions and RH Charles' edition of 1893 and his later revisions. The Genesis Apocryphon's version of Noah's birth (Qumran) is comparative only.
- *Classical ethnography and paradoxography.* Pliny and Solinus on the white-haired, pale-eyed Albani (including the claim of better sight at night) and on the *leucaethiopes*, considered where their reception in English can be shown, chiefly through the Middle English encyclopaedic channels above.
- *Latin and Anglo-Norman writing from England.* A small control set, not a systematic search, to test whether conventions for recording anomalous bodies differ from those of English-language writing (for example, twelfth-century Latin reports of the Green Children of Woolpit). The exclusion of Latin from the primary corpus is a practical limit and is acknowledged as such.

**Comparative (one chapter, reception and translation only).** Welsh, Irish and Norse material, treated in 4.6. This chapter is limited to a short list of cases, depends on specialist editions and translations, and the researcher's language competence will be stated plainly \[state competences - Old Norse is manageable with my knowledge of modern Scandinavian languages, Welsh/Irish would have to be assisted/through existing research\]. It will be the first strand reduced if the schedule requires.

**Not undertaken.** Systematic coverage of non-English originals; computational coverage of Old and Middle English; a second general history of albinism after 1650; and the twentieth century (see 4.7). The annotated dataset is a secondary output, released only where rights allow.

**Review point.** At the end of Year 1 the scale of the Middle English and comparative components is fixed on the basis of pilot results, and anything infeasible is dropped, not compressed.

### 4.6 Welsh, Irish and Norse traditions: reception and comparison

The comparative chapter tests a small number of cases against the definition in 4.1, and distinguishes a name or epithet from an explicit description of bodily appearance.

*Welsh.* Within *Culhwch ac Olwen*, Olwen is described with hair yellower than broom and skin whiter than the foam of the wave. This looks like conventional fair complexion and will be tested as a control, not assumed to be a core case. The white hounds with red ears in *Pwyll* are animals and fall outside the definition, though they may illuminate the pairing of white and red as an Otherworld marker. Gwyn ap Nudd, the Very White Witch and other figures are candidates only. The analysis will examine the semantic range of *gwyn/gwen* (whiteness, fairness, brightness, blessedness) and whether its use associates with beauty, heroism, supernatural status or liminality. It will not assume that Christianity created these associations, and will consider inherited vocabulary, Christian symbolism and the transformation of vernacular narrative as interacting possibilities.

*Irish.* Fionn/Find, whose hair is said to have turned white prematurely is a strong candidate for whiteness without an age cue. A route into English exists through Macpherson's Ossianic poems of the 1760s, a contested source that will be handled as evidence of eighteenth-century reception, not of Irish tradition.

*Norse.* Snorri describes Baldr as so fair of face and so bright that light shines from him, and names a very white plant after his brow. This is radiance and beauty, not necessarily white hair or skin, and the project will examine this alongside depictions in Vǫlundarkviða of "hvítan hals Völundar" (the white neck of Völundar) and Heimdallr, "hvítastr ása" (the whitest of the Æsir). Relations between Norse and Christian imagery, the etymology of Baldr's name, and differences between the poetic and Snorri's accounts and the semantics of the underlying terms are debated and will be handled through the secondary literature (see Section 5). Reception evidence includes Percy's English translation (1770) of Mallet's history of Denmark, later Eddic translations, and the medievalist writing of William Morris.

Reception evidence comprises texts demonstrably available to English readers; translated vocabulary is treated as evidence of translators' choices, not of the source tradition, and will be presented with a close reading of the term in context. Comparative material is used only to clarify a specific problem raised by the English evidence, and claims of common inheritance require evidence beyond resemblance of vocabulary or description.

### 4.7 Later appropriation

Twentieth-century racial-nationalist and occult appropriations of Norse imagery lie outside the period of study. They matter only as a reason for historicising meanings of whiteness instead of treating them as inherent. Goodrick-Clarke and Van Deusen \[complete citation\] provide relevant research. The project does not endorse such appropriations.

## 5. Existing scholarship

White's thesis is the principal point of departure for the history of albinism as a category. The present research uses it to identify sources and debates while avoiding a second general history over the same period. The difference is one of emphasis and object: the project begins from a broader and earlier set of bodily descriptions and asks how their meanings developed whether or not they were ever associated with albinism.

Charlotte Baker's work on representations of albinism in Francophone and African contexts shows the importance of examining representation within particular social and discursive settings and engages critical disability studies, which informs the treatment of stigma and embodiment. For Old English, Alaric Hall's *Elves in Anglo-Saxon England* (2007) offers a framework for supernatural beings, radiance and unusual bodies. Jackson Crawford's 2014 thesis, *The Historical Development of Basic Color Terms in Old Norse-Icelandic*, is a comparative resource where it bears on a defined problem.

The literature review will also establish scholarship on biblical reception, medieval and early modern descriptions of the body, historical colour terminology (including Biggam), and the emergence of medical understandings of albinism, organised around the central question rather than as separate surveys.

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Design

In addition to pre-determined texts, computational methods will identify candidate passages and reveal patterns hard to survey by reading alone. Interpretation then considers each passage's wording, genre, authorship, context and relationship to other sources. Embedding similarity, keyword frequency and cluster membership are not treated as evidence of meaning, they simply guide investigation so that literary-historical evidence determines what can be claimed.

### 6.2 Two-ended retrieval

- **Forward from bodily description.** Queries drawn from early anchors and related descriptions: white hair like wool or snow, very pale skin, pale or fiery eyes, radiance. Retrieved passages inform later queries.
- **Backward from the later category.** Searches begin from *albino* and the historical vocabulary that preceded and accompanied it ("white negro", *leucaethiops*, Dondos, *kakerlak*, and spelling variants), expanded through contextual vocabulary and neighbouring passages.

The two chains are investigated separately before comparison. Overlap identifies places for close reading; it does not establish historical connection. A claim of connection requires evidence such as identifiable quotation, allusion, translation, a shared authority or a demonstrable genre convention. Where only resemblance is established, the relationship remains unproven.

### 6.3 Passage-level retrieval and annotation

Retrieval operates on overlapping 40-word windows with a 20-word hop, so that each stretch of text appears in two windows and overlapping hits are merged for review, because pilot work suggests that relevant features are often distributed across several clauses. Each passage retains its bibliographic provenance and location so that it can be linked to the text in context. The annotation scheme distinguishes hair, skin, eyes, radiance and the function ascribed to the eyes; co-occurrence or independence of features; age cues and other explanations; the figure's status; genre and discourse; the presence and character of any recorded reaction; quotation, allusion or independent use; and confidence and interpretive uncertainty. It will be piloted and revised before full annotation, and borderline examples are retained, not forced into a binary.

### 6.4 Models and baselines

The encoder under evaluation is MacBERTh (Manjavacas and Fonteyn 2022), a BERT-base model pre-trained on English from 1450 to 1950. The current working proof of concept using an ensemble of vectors from various layers. Each passage vector is the unweighted mean of its 40 word vectors, which are averaged from subword states computed over 510-subword chunks of the whole document, so passages are embedded in document context, not in isolation. Layer eight, final-layer and mean-of-last-four-layers vectors are selected, and stored in a Lance index with date filtering. In addition to term and phrase search, query by example has proven effective. One worked example, starting from a twentieth-century medical passage on albinism, retrieved plausible earlier leads, including Middle English renderings of Revelation 1:14. No recall or precision has yet been measured and no comparison with keyword retrieval has been run, so its suitability for passage retrieval is a question to be answered, not assumed. The evaluation therefore should compare, on the same gold set (6.7) as many as feasible of:

1. a lexical baseline (BM25 and pattern matching over normalised and lemmatised text, with the hand-built lexicons);
2. a modern off-the-shelf sentence-embedding model, on lightly normalised text;
3. MacBERTh with the stored layer variants (layer 8, final layer, mean of the last four layers), with and without post-processing such as corpus-mean centring or whitening;
4. MacBERTh fine-tuned contrastively for sentence similarity, with the method to be chosen in the pilot, for example unsupervised or with pairs from the annotated sample;
5. a hybrid in which lexical retrieval secures recall and a dense or fine-tuned model reranks.

The method is adopted only if it demonstrably improves on the lexical baseline for this object. Because the prototype's neighbours include white animals as well as bodies (white hounds, a boar and a horse appeared beside the Apocalypse passage), a second stage will be needed to enforce the requirement in 4.1 that the described figure be human or human-like: annotation, or a classifier or reranker trained on the annotated sample. Several anchors, including the Wycliffite Bible phrase quoted above, predate 1450 and lie outside MacBERTh's training range. The prototype nonetheless retrieved a Middle English rendering of Revelation 1:14, so how well the model generalises to earlier English is treated as an empirical question: a pre-1450 subset of the gold set will compare it with the lexical baseline, and Middle English remains primarily a matter of lexical search and close reading. Early modern biblical translations of the same verses serve as in-range queries. Overlap between MacBERTh's pre-training data and the corpora searched will be documented. Time slicing is not envisaged as necessary for a model with such a wide training set. If results volume is excessive, clustering may be used to organise results by genre/semantic field. As a complement to passage retrieval, token-level MacBERTh vectors may be stored for a small set of target words (white/whyte, hair/heer, wool/wolle, eye) and clustered by sense in context, which draws on the model's published strength in word-level tasks.

Old and Middle English are investigated by lexical and collocational methods and close reading, not represented as having equivalent model coverage.

### 6.6 Quotation and reception

Biblical and other identifiable quotations are not discarded as noise but taken as evidence of reception and the continued availability of particular images. Passages are annotated as quotation, allusion or independent use where this can be established, and summaries distinguish results that include quotations from those that exclude them, to assess whether continuity reflects repeated transmission, convention or independent description.

### 6.7 Validation

A hand-annotated sample assesses whether retrieval identifies passages relevant to the object. Precision is examined by period and, where samples allow, by feature and genre. Recall is addressed through a gold set built from known cases: the passages in White's thesis for 1650-1914, the anchor passages named in this outline, and passages found independently by close reading. Where feasible a second annotator assesses a subset and the limits of agreement are reported. False positives and borderline cases are analysed as evidence about the method and the definition of the object. The annotated sample is a research output in its own right.

### 6.8 Selection and the recording of reactions

The surviving record may preserve unusual encounters because they provoked a response. The project separates whether a text records a reaction to a described body from what kind of reaction it records (astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust, theological or medical interpretation). More mundane, matter-of-fact descriptions receive particular attention as a counterpoint to symbolic or sensational accounts. The frequency of recorded reactions can be measured in the retrieved material - the probability that an ordinary encounter was recorded at all cannot, and it is highlighted that the surviving record is not to be treated as a representative sample of lived encounters.

## 7. Researcher position and reflexivity

The project is undertaken by a researcher who himself has albinism. This informs the choice of questions and an awareness of the consequences of historical representation, but does not determine the meanings found in the evidence. The project does not assume that albinism or extraordinary paleness has an inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is historical: the meanings that writers, communities and traditions attached to visibly marked bodies. Evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own judgement are kept distinct.

### 8.2 Outputs and beneficiaries

The principal output is a PhD thesis. Secondary outputs, subject to copyright and licensing, are an annotated passage collection, a reproducible retrieval pipeline and articles for digital humanities, literary and disability studies; any release will document provenance, annotation decisions and limitations.

The contribution is mainly scholarly, but the research should also be relevant to people with albinism and their families, educators, and organisations concerned with albinism and minority representation. It will show that the historical record holds a wider range of representations than the negative ones most familiar in modern accounts, and supports White's argument that albinism is a historically "term used in Western medicine and ophthalmology," with its own "specific history and etymology," which should not by itself define the identity of those classified by it (White 2011, p. 14). Findings will be shared in accessible language, and the project will consider consulting organisations such as Standing Voice and national support groups on presentation.

## 9. Risks and limitations

- **Breadth.** The topic touches many traditions. Mitigation: the core, contextual and excluded strands in 4.5, a Year 1 review, and a comparative chapter that can be reduced.
- **Method performance.** MacBERTh may not outperform a lexical baseline for this object. Mitigation: baselines, a hybrid design, and a thesis whose claims rest on close reading, with retrieval as support.
- **Sparse evidence.** The phenomenon may be rare or unevenly distributed. Mitigation: passage-level retrieval, cautious lexicon expansion, and close reading where counts are low.
- **Corpus and OCR bias.** EEBO-TCP is hand-keyed, but ECCO and nineteenth-century material carry OCR error, and spelling varies. Mitigation: preliminary work suggests clustering MacBERTh vectors minimises the impact of orthographic variation and OCR noise; document coverage, inspection of sources and light normalisation increase the improvement.
- **An unstable object.** Age-related whiteness and the core/associated distinction may stay ambiguous. Mitigation: borderline categories and sensitivity tests of alternative inclusion decisions.
- **False continuity.** Similar descriptions may be mistaken for transmission. Mitigation: separating resemblance from documented reception, and leaving unresolved relationships unresolved but highlighted.
- **Circularity.** The seed descriptions shape what the system finds. Mitigation: two-ended retrieval, controls, analysis of false positives and validation by close reading.
- **Limits of the record.** Texts cannot show how representative their depictions were of ordinary encounters.
- **Language competence.** Old English, Latin and the comparative languages are read at different levels. Mitigation: the researcher has some competency in German, Scandinavian languages, Middle and Old English, is comfortable with specialist editions and linguistic analysis of Germanic languages; specialist advice will be sought where necessary, particularly for any Old Welsh and Old Irish.
