# The central distinction emerging

The evidence seems to divide into **two fairly different systems**.

### A. Light/white appearance **without age**

| Form            | What is marked?                       |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **hwītloc**     | White/fair/light hair                 |
| **hwītloccedu** | A white/fair-haired woman             |
| **hwīt**        | Basic whiteness, brightness, fairness |
| **blāc**        | Pallor/brightness/deathly lightness   |

### B. White/grey appearance **through age**

| Form              | What is marked?                     |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **hār**           | Hoary/grey-white                    |
| **feaxhār**       | Hoary-haired                        |
| **blondenfeax**   | Mixed/grizzled/greying hair         |
| **gamolfeax**     | Old/grey-haired                     |
| **hār hilderinc** | The conventional aged/hoary warrior |

This is, I think, the **most important philological result so far**:

> **Old English does not simply use one concept of “white hair.”**

It distinguishes—or at least lexicalises differently—**light/fair hair** and **the hoary whiteness of age**.

# The elf evidence: a separate layer

Now adding **Alaric Hall** and *ælf*, we need a different evidential status.

| Claim / evidence                                                        | Status                                              | What it contributes                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **OE ælf** denotes an otherworldly being                                | **Explicit OE evidence**                            | The basic supernatural category                                                 |
| Early Anglo-Saxon elves were broadly human-like                         | **Hall's reconstruction/interpretation**            | Makes bodily appearance potentially meaningful                                  |
| *ælf* is etymologically connected with a root associated with whiteness | **Strong but historically reconstructed etymology** | Raises the possibility of “white/fair ones”                                     |
| The etymological connection necessarily means “shining being”           | **Not established**                                 | Hall cautions against this simplification                                       |
| Whiteness may connect to beauty                                         | **Important interpretative argument in Hall**       | Provides a possible cultural bridge                                             |
| Elves were explicitly described in OE as white-haired or white-skinned  | **Not currently evidenced**                         | We must not claim this                                                          |
| Elves were albino-like                                                  | **Unsupported**                                     | This would be an anachronistic overreach                                        |
| Later Scandinavian **ljósálfar** are explicitly “light-elves”           | **Explicit later Scandinavian evidence**            | Relevant comparative evidence, but cannot simply be projected backwards onto OE |

The **question marks are important**. The lower connection—**light/white → beauty → ælf**—is not something the OE corpus itself explicitly states. It is a possible synthesis of:

1. OE **hwītloccedu** and light-haired feminine description;
2. Hall's work on **beautiful and female elves**;
3. the etymological association of **ælf** with whiteness.

## The strongest pieces of evidence so far

If we ranked them specifically for your project on **extreme bodily whiteness**, I would currently put them like this:

### **Tier 1 — direct evidence**

1. **hwītloc / hwītloccedu**
   Explicitly light/white hair, **without old age being encoded**.

2. **hār / feaxhār / gamolfeax / blondenfeax**
   Explicitly white/grey hair as an **age-marked bodily phenomenon**.

3. **blāc**
   A potentially crucial alternative vocabulary for **pallor and deathly/extreme bodily lightness**.

### **Tier 2 — structural/contextual evidence**

4. **White/fair hair + high-status feminine description** in *hwītloccedu*.

5. **The systematic distinction between fair/light and hoary/aged whiteness**.

6. The extension of **hār** from hair to weathered/aged non-human surfaces.

### **Tier 3 — etymological/mythological evidence**

7. **ælf** and its possible inherited association with whiteness.

8. Hall's reconstruction of elves as broadly **human-like otherworldly beings**.

9. Hall's exploration of **beauty, gender and whiteness**, rather than simply treating elves as luminous beings.

10. Later **ljósálfar** evidence as a comparative—but chronologically separate—Germanic development.

## The working hypothesis I think is now worth testing

We can formulate the research problem much more precisely:

> **Does early Germanic and Old English tradition distinguish ordinary age-related whiteness from a separate category of conspicuous fair/light bodily appearance, and does the latter become associated with beauty, aristocratic distinction, supernatural otherness, or non-human identity?**

That is a substantially stronger and more testable question than simply:

> “Were white-haired people represented in Old English?”

Because, so far, the answer to the latter is straightforward: **yes, overwhelmingly as old people**.

The interesting question is what happens to **whiteness when age does *not* explain it**.

And that is precisely where **hwītloccedu**, **blāc**, and potentially **ælf** become disproportionately important.
